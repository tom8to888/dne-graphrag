Introduction
Through a simple search on academic databases,
one can easily see that there is little to no scholarly
work regarding wheelchair tennis coaching. When
confronting that idea with the notion that wheelchair
tennis is the most professional and inclusive sport
among other parasports, it is apparent that more
work must be done to create awareness. Of all the
parasports, wheelchair tennis has a pathway from
beginner to Grand Slam and Paralympic champion.
With a worldwide professional tour managed by the
International Tennis Federation, there are more
than 150 professional tournaments with more than
$5 million USD in prize money. Moreover, the only
adaptation for wheelchair tennis is that athletes get
two bounces. Thus, it is easily accessible, has an entire
pathway, with worldwide resources for athletes to
aspire to greatness.
This study intended to glean insights from five
performance wheelchair tennis coaches that are
current national coaches for Team USA Wheelchair
Tennis at the United States Tennis Association. The
main idea was to determine what good coaching is in
wheelchair tennis at the elite level. One-hour, semiWorld Tennis Coaching & Sport Scienci Review

Abstract
This study intended to address the notion of what is good coaching in wheelchair tennis at the elite level.
One of the problems is that there is a great deal of wheelchair tennis coaches but very little literature
on the subject. This qualitative research used purposeful sampling to choose five United States Tennis
Association National Wheelchair Tennis Coaches, including the head coach of Team USA. One-hour
semi-structured interviews, with ten pre-prepared questions, took place over Zoom and Otter.ai was
used to transcribe. Transcriptions were reviewed, and both codes and themes were derived. One of
the major findings of this research was that there are no formal education opportunities in wheelchair
tennis coaching, and the usual way most coaches learn is through informal education, specifically
mentorship. There were several other findings which contribute meaningfully to this newer subject and
hopefully, more research will occur.
Key words: wheelchair tennis; formal education, mentorship, potential barriers.

Discussion of the findings
Ten overarching themes were discussed by the five
High-Performance Coaches based on broad, openended questions to ascertain opinions on best
coaching practices. The themes were: best coaching
practices in wheelchair tennis, potential barriers,
formal education experiences, athlete perception
of best coaching practices, experiences in formal/
informal/non-formal education, international best
coaching practices, detrimental practices, mentoring,
reciprocal mentorship, and improving coaching
practices. Various codes emerged in each theme and
will be detailed in the following section. A number of
instances in which each code was mentioned will be
shown in parentheses.
Best Coaching Practices
All interviewees agreed that conventional tennis
knowledge was sufficient for a successful wheelchair
tennis coach. However, it was concluded that what a
coach needs to learn, specifically to wheelchair tennis,
revolves
around
disability-specific
information,
chair setup, and mobility. Notions on disability
were mentioned 29 times during data collection.
One interviewee stated, “The better you understand
disability, the better you understand the player’s
potential.” One's disability will inform how a chair
needs to be configured, which also informs one's
ability to propel the chair. Other standard practices
among sports, such as creating a culture (7), being
dedicated (8), foundational coaching knowledge (24),
and being a strong communicator (7), were identified.
Potential Barriers
One of the identified problems in attracting and
retaining new wheelchair tennis players is the lack
of qualified coaches. Thus, interviewees were asked
to identify the existing barriers to attracting newly
certified tennis professionals. All agreed that the
number one obstacle was finding qualified mentors
(15). One participant specifically said, “I think the first
barrier is just finding somebody else that can dedicate
their time to teaching the next coach to do it and being
a mentor for them. That knowledge needs to be passed
on before it's lost.” This, in turn, also contributed to
substantial fears (7) that prospective coaches may
have. It was relayed that tennis coaches typically feel
the need to be an expert in what they are coaching.
This feeling contributes to higher self-efficacy and a
more substantial clientele base. When there is fear or
low self-efficacy, it leads to a lack of commitment or
a high turnover rate. Other notable barriers were the
lack of financial viability (5) and a significant gap in
academic research (4).
Formal Education
This theme arose due to the controversy surrounding
an overall lack of formal education opportunities
interviewee commented, “the off-court practices of
enabling is not good. It usually comes from a caring
place, but it takes away from an athlete’s ability to
be independent and self-advocate.” They observed
athletes being degraded, receiving overtly negative
feedback, and an overwhelming culture of enabling.
One of the detrimental fundamental principles is
when a coach also serves as a caretaker, which leads to
a culture of enabling. Wheelchair athletes report that
they want to be treated as equal athletes. So, coaches
must emphasize independence and self-advocacy.
With trust being reported as key to the coach/athlete
relationship, interviewees said that being too critical
and enabling can lead to mistrust.
Mentoring
Although mentorship is mentioned in other sections,
it stood out as a theme on its own. National coaches
all agreed that mentorship (10) from other successful
coaches and players was the only current viable form
of coaching education. One relayed, “I have been able
to take it to a higher level, from a coaching standpoint,
because of my mentors.” However, it was reported that
a formal mentorship program should also be instituted
with efforts to add wheelchair tennis coaching to the
formal education pathway. It was relayed that there
was fear that some of the most knowledgeable mentors
were aging out. The immense knowledge will soon be
lost if a formalized process does not occur quickly.
Reciprocal Mentorship
This term kept coming up throughout the five
interviews. Allen and Douglas (2021) published an
article on reciprocal mentorship in wheelchair tennis.
The researchers pointed out that it is a unique concept
in parasport coaching that can lead to better attraction
and retention of qualified coaches. All respondents
agreed that it was critical to coaching wheelchair
tennis (5). Prospective coaches will experience less
intimidation if they know that a wheelchair athlete will
be open and able to discuss disability, chair setup, and
mobility patterns. Over a period of time, as the tennis
coach learns more about wheelchair tennis, both the
coach and player can return to their traditional roles.
According to the interviewees, reciprocal mentorship
engenders quicker disability-specific knowledge (5),
good communication (5), and creates mutual trust
(27).

International Coaches’ Best Practices
The interviewees have extensive travel experience
as they have all taken part in various international
competitions worldwide. Therefore, their opinion
was asked about the best coaching practices observed
by noteworthy international coaches. The main
observation was that the best coaches embody
professionalism and intensity (5). Observations
included rigorous notetaking, scouting, high-intensity
training sessions, and more live-ball practice (2).
One coach said, “the international coaches have very
high standards and they hold players accountable.”
Interviewees also relayed that in the past, American
coaches spent too much time with "fed ball" (2) (feeding
a ball is also known as dead-ball). It is when a coach
simply hits a ball from their hand to a specific spot and
does not play out the point. The best international
coaches tend to recreate more live-ball (2) situations
for athletes to practice pressure situations.

How to Improve Coaching?
As the five interviewees collectively represented
111 years of coaching experience, they were asked
to comment on ways to strengthen wheelchair
tennis coaching practices. The two most cited
recommendations were a more formalized mentorship
program (5) and a formal education approach (5).
Both recommendations complement one another
as a formal mentorship program is typically part of
most sports' formal education programming. One
interviewee echoed this with the following statement,
“I think the USPTA and PTR need to continue to require
that this becomes part of our certification, because the
more we see it, the better coaches will be and the more
comfortable they will be.” It was also reported that
much more work must be done to create awareness (5)
about wheelchair tennis, which will lead to less fear
(3). In conclusion, more education opportunities were
paramount to continued growth in the sport.
Conclusion
This study sheds a great deal of light on wheelchair
tennis coaching from five extremely qualified and
experienced performance coaches. Each of these
coaches has worked with and produced Grand Slam
champions,
Paralympic
champions,
and
World
Champions. It was important to memorialize their
collective thoughts to help answer the question of
what good coaching looks like in wheelchair tennis.
They were asked ten questions during a semistructured one-hour interview. Various themes,
mentioned above in sections, shed light on prevalent
coaching considerations for tennis coaches desirous
of working with wheelchair tennis athletes. This study
will hopefully spark more interest in this topic, which
will lead to more work being done to fill the gap in
academic literature.
