Introduction
Wheelchair tennis, a rapidly growing adaptive sport,
presents unique coaching challenges that differentiate
it from conventional tennis instruction. Despite its
increasing popularity and inclusion in major national
tennis organizations, there remains a notable lack
of formalized educational resources and evidencebased best practices, particularly for coaches working
with beginner and intermediate-level athletes. This
study addresses that gap by exploring the practical
experiences of certified tennis professionals who
have specialized in wheelchair tennis coaching.
Drawing on the expertise of coaches across various
U.S. regions, this research aims to identify and codify
the most effective coaching strategies, equipment
considerations, and player engagement techniques
essential for developing entry-level wheelchair tennis
players.

Abstract
The purpose of this study was to identify best coaching practices for beginner and intermediate
wheelchair tennis players. A focus group, comprising eight certified tennis coaches with wheelchair
tennis expertise, was conducted using a semi-structured, 10-question interview format. Participants
were purposefully sampled from eight USTA sections across the United States. Data were collected via
video-conferencing, transcribed using voice-to-text software, and analyzed through thematic coding,
resulting in nine overall themes: equipment, engagement in group lessons, engagement in private
lessons, disability and lesson planning, challenges with group and private lessons, best practices in
player development, chair setup, and improving coaching. Key findings included the critical importance
of proper adjustable tennis wheelchairs and modified equipment for stroke production and mobility.
For engagement, competitive, games-based coaching and detailed, long-term lesson planning were
highlighted. The study also revealed challenges related to adapting lessons for varying disabilities and
ensuring safety. A lack of formal educational resources and mentoring opportunities were identified
as major barriers to improving coaching practices. The findings provide specific recommendations for
coaches and organizations seeking to enhance the quality of beginner and intermediate wheelchair
tennis instruction.
Key words: wheelchair tennis, tennis wheelchairs, modified equipment, adaptive coaching

Procedures
The approach used to collect data in this study was
a focus group. Purposeful sampling was used to
identify eight wheelchair tennis coaches who work
with beginner to intermediate athletes (Claxton &
Michael, 2020). Coaches were explicitly chosen from
eight regions of the United States delineated by USTA
sections (Get to Know the USTA Sections, n.d.). Those
sections were Florida, Southern, Midwest, Southern
California, Intermountain, Texas, Southwest, and New
England. Interviewees were coaches who had added
wheelchair tennis as a specialty to their existing
tennis professional certification (PTR - Professional
Tennis Registry, n.d.; USPTA Members Home, n.d.).
Thus, they were certified conventional tennis coaches
who had also taken the only known wheelchair tennis
coach development workshop provided by the USTA
(USTA Wheelchair Tennis Coaching, n.d.).
The focus group comprised of a face-to-face,
10-question, semi-structured interview that allowed
for follow-up questions (Claxton & Michael, 2020, p.
55). After obtaining consent from the participants,
it was conducted at the USTA National Campus
in Orlando, Florida, and used video-conferencing
powered by Zoom software. The focus group was
recorded, lasted 96 minutes, and responses were
transcribed using Otter.ai, voice-to-text software.
Coding was used to establish themes. Data were
organized using a codes and themes table to easily
contribute to a list of best practices in wheelchair
tennis coaching (Claxton & Michael, 2020, p. 65).
Questions were predetermined and created based on
existing academic literature.

Literature review
A significant gap exists in empirical research
specifically
addressing

and

parasport literature has expanded, existing studies
remain

focused
on
medical,
psychological,

high-performance

rather than pedagogical methodology (Townsend

adaptive sports relies almost exclusively on informal,
experiential learning characterized by trial and error
(Bentzen et al., 2021; Townsend et al., 2021). This lack
of structured, formal educational frameworks breeds
low self-efficacy, hesitation, and anxiety among
coaches transitioning from able-bodied sports (Judge
et al., 2021; Macdonald et al., 2016). This dynamic
ultimately hinders both athlete recruitment and
sustained tournament participation (McKenzie et al.,
2021).
To

of informal and non-formal educational techniques
into formal sport-specific pathways (Bentzen et al.,
2021). A primary mechanism for boosting coach
self-efficacy
is
reciprocal
mentorship,
wherein
experienced
wheelchair
tennis
players
mentor
certified able-bodied coaches (Allen & Douglas, 2021).
This collaborative approach effectively bridges the
knowledge deficit that certified tennis coaches face
regarding disability-specific dynamics, equipment
configurations, movement patterns, and psychosocial
factors (Allen & Douglas, 2021). Furthermore,
Townsend et al. (2021) advocates for a critical shift
toward a specialized disability coaching education
framework that leverages these established athletecentered pedagogical principles.
Structurally, national governing bodies are increasingly
pressured to integrate adaptive sports within existing
athlete development models (Townsend et al., 2021).
For example, the United States Tennis Association
adapted the American Development Model to include
dedicated curricula focused on learner-centered
coaching, instructional differentiation, and stageappropriate modified equipment (Davies, 2017, 2020).
Finally, establishing formal Communities of Practice

6. What are the challenges associated with
coaching private lessons in wheelchair tennis?
The purpose of this question was to collect feedback on
complex aspects of coaching wheelchair tennis players
individually. Douglas et al. (2018) reported that due to
a lack of formal education opportunities in parasport
coaching, most learning occurs through non-formal
or informal opportunities. Thus, it is possible that
coaches do not have appropriate training in general
best education practices. Answers to this question
gave specific data that informed recommendations for
best coaching practices in individual lessons.
7. Describe best practices for player development
in coaching wheelchair tennis.
Sanchez-Pay and Sanz-Rivas (2020) conducted a
narrative review in wheelchair tennis by analyzing the
small number of publications on the sport since 1980.
This open-ended question sought to gain feedback on
how to progress wheelchair tennis players along the
pathway from beginner to advanced. Although some
coaches do not intend to progress wheelchair tennis
players to higher levels, it is generally understood that
both coaches and players aspire to continue training
for mastery to achieve a higher level. Thus, the authors
highlighted that most literature is concerned with
advancing players to Paralympic levels of play.

2. How do you keep athletes engaged during
group lessons?
This question sought to identify specific teaching
techniques that enhanced athlete engagement.
According to Davies (2017), traditional tennis coaching
was not conducive to athlete achievement. In the
past, students were primarily engaged in line drills
where all would have to wait an excessive amount of
time before they got a chance to hit another ball. In
essence, tennis players would hit one shot, then move
to the end of the line and wait their turn. Although
line drills promote organization, they represent a
coach-centered teaching approach instead of athletecentered coaching. Davies recommends conducting
more live-ball play opportunities so that athletes
stay engaged and participate in cooperative learning
activities.
3. How do you keep an athlete engaged during
private lessons?
 This question aimed to analyze techniques to keep
athletes engaged while working one on one with
a coach. As the literature illustrates, there are few
formal education opportunities in wheelchair tennis
(Bentzen et al., 2021). Moreover, Dr. Paul Roetert,
Managing Director of USTA Coaching Education,
pointed out a lack of proven education best practices
in general tennis coaching (personal communication,
July 30, 2021). Whitaker (2020) examined nineteen
best teaching practices that, although in the
classroom, could be used effectively in wheelchair
tennis coaching. Therefore, this interview question
presented an opportunity to compare successful
individual engagement techniques with proven best
education practices.

Equipment
According to the participants, this theme was a
critical element to coaching properly. One of the
most discussed issues was the tennis wheelchair (35).
Without the right chair and its precise adjustments
depending on the athlete's needs, students will not be
able to progress. It was agreed that an adjustable chair
was optimal for new players and younger people who
are still developing physically. Fixed frames would be
appropriate once an athlete specializes in the sport
and is done growing.
The use of modified equipment (22) and disabilityspecific devices (8) was also discussed in detail. All
agreed that transition equipment such as smaller
court sizes, smaller racquets, and lower compression
balls was of the utmost importance for proper stroke
production and mobility (22). One coach mentioned,
“we use the transition tennis balls mainly, but the red
ball presents some difficulty as it does not bounce
very high and is hard to grab. Eventually, when they
are ready for tournaments, we use yellow ball. For
players with limited hand strength, we will tape.”
It leads to age and stage-appropriate development
that creates more opportunities for fun. Disabilityspecific equipment such as athletic tape, straps, and
belts was also identified. For those with less function,
impaired balance, and limited hand strength, taping
the racquet to one's hand and using straps for balance
also increased player ability and enjoyment.
Interviewees
highlighted
creative
approaches
to problem solving. One said, “for grassroots
programming, I've learned to go to Walmart and grab
a couple weightlifting belts for straps. I never know
what kind of players are coming in and out with all
different levels of disability, so I have various types of
straps for those that have balance issues. I also always
have athletic tape for taping the racket to the hand.”
The importance of the correct-sized sports wheelchair
was also mentioned. One interviewee said, “more
junior chairs, usually they get in a chair and the chairs
are too big for them.”

Focus group findings
The focus group was conducted with eight certified
recreational wheelchair tennis coaches to ascertain
the best coaching practices specific to beginner and
intermediate level players. All coaches were certified
by either the PTR or the USPTA. The ages ranged from
35 to 55 years. They were chosen from eight different
sections from the USTA (Southern, Eastern, Midwest,
Southern California, Intermountain, Florida, Missouri
Valley, and Northern) They were specifically identified
in conjunction with USTA National Wheelchair Tennis
staff based on their proven experience in coaching
and existing programs in their region. The interviewer
took notes to facilitate follow-up questions during
the focus group. After the focus group session,
the transcription was reviewed, and themes were
established based on the ten questions. Codes emerged
from each question and were analyzed by creating a
table in Microsoft Word. The table is comprehensive
and far too large for publication. However, it can be
requested by correspondence with the author.

Best Practices in Player Development
In most sports, the term player development typically
connotes high-performance training. However, in
this context, members were asked to speak about
specific topics essential to developing players from
the beginning and intermediate stages. The most
Respondents shared that people generally enjoy
playing. Therefore, when players use age and stageappropriate equipment, it promotes more point play
and longer rallies. Spending more time on lesson
planning for group lessons stood out (9). Coaches
reported that it was necessary to design a series
of group lessons. It was also reported that letting
students lead (5) certain activities was beneficial for
more buy-in and station work led to more engagement
as students experienced less downtime. Finally, music
(1) was identified as a way to relax the environment
and increase the fun.
Engagement in Private Lessons
Participants spent most of the time focusing on
lesson planning during the private lesson portion of
the focus group. It was highlighted that individual
player
development
required
more
short
and
long-term planning (23). Doing so requires more
frequent assessments and reflection (4) to prepare
for discussions with the athlete. Participants shared
that in individual lessons, students engage more in
asking high order questions regarding the reasoning
behind specific drills, activities, live-ball, fed-ball,
athletic development, and competition. Coaches must
demonstrate that they care about the athlete and are
willing to put the extra time in to prepare. In doing so,
athletes will be more engaged in their development.
An interviewee explained, “privates are the best time
to go after individualized goals and individualized
projects. There should be an assessment period
followed by goal setting.”
Disability and Lesson Planning
Disability-specific knowledge (8) is essential to
coaching wheelchair tennis players. Yet, participants
spoke more about the specific time they take to
properly assess one's disability as there tends to be
more variation in function at the recreational levels.
A participant mentioned, “I immediately look at
how their chair is set up. It tells me a lot about their
disability. I also notice any extra strapping.” Athletes
can vary from amputees, paraplegic, quadriplegic,
congenital disabilities, with a combination of
cognitive and physical impairments. Athletes can also
show up to play in everyday chairs, adjustable frames,
fixed frames, and even power chairs. Once there is a
determination made of one's ability and the proper
equipment has been identified, coaches reported that
it was then incumbent upon them to try and challenge
players (3) to do more than they think is possible.
Challenges with Group and Private Lessons
As participants in this focus group work extensively
with group and private lessons, they were asked to
opine on specific challenges in each. One's ability
to constantly make adaptations (3) for current and
prospective wheelchair athletes was reported as the
main challenge. A coach noted, “there are always
a one-stop-shop online for videos, curricula, tips,
online courses, and more. A coach said, “so I really
think that we should always have the resources online
to share with anyone who's interested. We also need
more mentoring opportunities. If there was a library
of online resources or a one-stop-shop, that would be
very helpful.” Another coach agreed and highlighted
mentoring, “we need more mentoring. We also need
much more educational resources like videos, photos,
and diagrams.”
Limitations and further study
Several limitations in the present study should be
noted, which concurrently offer valuable avenues for
future inquiry. First, this research relied on a single
focus group consisting of a relatively small sample size
(N = 8) of certified tennis coaches. While purposeful
sampling ensured geographical diversity across eight
distinct USTA sections, the small cohort restricts
the generalizability of the findings to the broader
population of adaptive sports educators across the
United States. Future research should replicate
this design using a larger, multi-center sample or
quantitative surveys to validate these themes on a
national scale.
Second, the study utilized a qualitative framework based
on self-reported insights from video-conferencing
software. Self-reported data inherently introduces
potential response, recall, or social desirability biases,
as coaches may overreport positive strategies or
misremember specific lesson dynamics. Furthermore,
this study prioritized the perspectives of conventional
able-bodied certified tennis coaches who transitioned
into adaptive coaching. This focus inherently omitted
the direct lived experiences of recreational wheelchair
tennis athletes themselves, whose unique viewpoints
are crucial for confirming coaching efficacy. Future
studies would benefit from incorporating triangulated
methodologies, including direct on-court behavioral
observations and qualitative interviews with entrylevel and intermediate wheelchair athletes.
Finally, the scope of this study was limited to identifying
broad best practices for beginner and intermediate
recreational players. Consequently, the research did
not deeply investigate the nuances of coaching athletes
with specific, overlapping cognitive impairments, nor
did it structurally analyze the mechanical differences
between specific equipment setups (e.g., fixed vs.
adjustable frames). An essential avenue for further
study involves creating and evaluating specific,
formalized coach education curricula based on these
findings. Future inquiry should explicitly track the
longitudinal efficacy of these targeted educational
resources and online repositories on coach selfefficacy and athlete retention rates.

Chair Setup
Although participants mentioned specifics regarding
wheelchairs in a previous question about equipment,
it was still essential to obtain responses on chair
expertise. Of the eight focus group members, two
are wheelchair users in everyday life. The other six
reported that detailed wheelchair setup knowledge is
challenging to master, while the two chair users said
it was not challenging. Most able-bodied wheelchair
tennis coaches lean heavily on the more experienced
players in the local program, or they call mentors for
extra help (3). Everyone still agreed that the use of an
adjustable wheelchair was paramount for beginning to
intermediate players (7). The move towards purchasing
a fixed frame should only occur after significant
commitment to the sport and athletes have stopped
growing physically. One coach mentioned, “I always
encourage new players to get an adjustable frame so
that we can play around and find their sweet spot.”

Conclusion
This qualitative study successfully addressed the
critical gap in formalized educational resources for
beginner and intermediate wheelchair tennis coaches
by synthesizing the practical expertise of eight certified
tennis professionals. Through a semi-structured
focus group, the research yielded rich qualitative
data, revealing nine overarching themes essential for
effective coaching: equipment, engagement in group
and private lessons, disability and lesson planning,
challenges in group and private lessons, best practices
in player development, chair setup, and improving
coaching practices. The findings underscore the
specialized nature of adaptive sports instruction,
particularly the paramount importance of appropriate,
adjustable equipment and the necessity for highly
individualized, planned instruction that accounts
for diverse physical and cognitive impairments. The
prominence of themes such as "Have a Plan" and the
continual need for "Adapting" confirms that successful
coaching in this domain moves beyond general tennis
pedagogy, demanding an athlete-centered approach
rooted in flexibility, assessment, and long-term
developmental strategy.
Crucially, the collective recommendations from the
participants, foremost among them the creation
of "More Educational Resources," provide a clear
mandate for advancing the field. By codifying lived
experiences into practical insights, this research
furnishes a foundational evidence base that can
directly inform the development of comprehensive,
standardized coach education curricula. Ultimately,
the synthesis of these expert perspectives offers not
only a set of best practices but also a blueprint for
fostering a more consistent, qualified, and inclusive
coaching environment, thereby enhancing the quality
of experience and developmental pathways for entrylevel and intermediate wheelchair tennis athletes.
