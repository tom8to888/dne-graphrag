Introduction

2016). This tension between participation growth and
pathway misalignment raises fundamental questions
about how talent is conceptualised, identified, and
supported within wheelchair tennis. Traditional
talent development models in tennis emphasise

Abstract
Wheelchair tennis has grown rapidly as a high‑performance sport, yet its talent development pathways
remain fragmented, unevenly resourced, and insufficiently informed by contemporary athlete
development theory. This opinion paper argues that current models, often adapted from non-disabled
tennis or general disability sport, fail to account for the unique biopsychosocial realities of wheelchair
athletes. Drawing on conceptual frameworks from talent development, ecological dynamics, disability
studies, and wheelchair‑specific performance research, the paper critiques prevailing assumptions
about early identification, classification, and performance potential. It proposes a shift toward inclusive,
context‑sensitive, and evidence‑informed pathways that recognise the diversity of impairment types,
the prevalence of late entry, and the centrality of equipment, environment, and social support. In
doing so, the paper positions wheelchair tennis as a case through which broader assumptions in talent
development can be critically examined, particularly where late entry, impairment‑specific constraints,
and fragmented access challenge conventional pathway logic. Practical implications are offered
through example drills, coaching strategies, and structural recommendations. The paper concludes
that wheelchair tennis requires a bespoke, athlete‑centred talent model that values adaptability,
opportunity, and long‑term engagement over narrow performance metrics.
Key words: wheelchair tennis; talent development; disability sport; athlete pathways.

This paper argues that wheelchair tennis consequently
requires a fundamental rethinking of its talent pathways.
Rather than retrofitting non-disabled models, the
sport should adopt an inclusive, evidence‑informed
approach that explicitly recognises the interaction
between impairment, equipment, environment, and
psychosocial factors (Martin, 2017; Shakespeare,
2014). Although wheelchair tennis performance
research has expanded in recent years, applied
findings relating to court movement, propulsion
demands, and learning constraints remain unevenly
integrated into development systems (Sindall, 2016).
Recent reviews further indicate fragmentation across
domains such as physical demands, injury risk,
psychosocial outcomes, and performance monitoring,
reinforcing the need for integrated pathway design
(Williamson et al., 2024; Leale et al., 2025). Similar
misalignments between theory and applied coaching
practice have been identified in other individual
sports. For example, analysis of elite squash training
environments revealed a persistent reliance on
traditional practice regimes that were poorly aligned
with established skill‑acquisition frameworks, despite
a strong theoretical evidence base (Mounter et al.,
2025). This pattern suggests that the challenge lies less
in the absence of theory and more in how development
systems conceptualise and operationalise it—an issue
likely to be magnified in wheelchair tennis given
additional impairment, equipment, and access‑related
constraints. Rather than seeking to generate new
empirical data, this paper therefore focuses on
clarifying how existing theory and applied evidence
have been interpreted, operationalised, and, in some
cases, misapplied within current wheelchair tennis
development systems.
Methods and Procedures
This paper adopts a conceptual synthesis methodology
to generate theoretical insight in an area where
empirical
evidence
is
expanding
but
remains
fragmented. Literature was purposefully reviewed
across four domains relevant to wheelchair tennis
development: performance science, classification
and impairment research, talent development and
coaching science, and disability sport psychosocial
literature. Sources were selected based on relevance,
conceptual
contribution,
influence
within
their respective fields. In addition to peer‑reviewed
research, applied technical reports and doctoral work
were included where they provided sport‑specific
insight into wheelchair tennis performance, skill
acquisition, and physical demands (e.g. Sindall, 2016;
Mayrhuber et al., 2022). Using a qualitative thematic
approach, the literature was analysed to identify
recurring concepts, contradictions, and gaps. This
process informed the development of a wheelchair
tennis‑specific framework emphasising the athlete–
chair–environment system (Davids et al., 2013), the
interaction between impairment and equipment,
and the role of social and structural constraints.
The resulting framework provides a structured basis
for interpreting how developmental assumptions
manifest across pathway design, coaching practice,
and athlete experience in wheelchair tennis.
Results
The conceptual synthesis identified four interrelated
findings that highlight limitations within existing
wheelchair tennis talent pathways and reinforce the
need for sport‑specific development models. These
findings relate to (i) structural misalignment with
common athlete entry profiles, (ii) the developmental
significance
of
impairment
classification,
(iii) the distinct skill‑acquisition demands of the
athlete–chair–environment system, and (iv) the
disproportionate influence of social and environmental
constraints.
First, current talent pathways remain structurally
misaligned with the developmental realities of
wheelchair tennis athletes. Many systems continue
to
assume
early
specialisation,
uninterrupted
progression, and high training volumes beginning in
childhood—assumptions that reflect non-disabled
tennis norms rather than typical wheelchair tennis
entry pathways. In practice, many athletes enter the
sport later following injury, illness, or rehabilitation,
resulting in non‑linear developmental trajectories
and variable access to coaching, equipment, and
competition (Dehghansai et al., 2017). Second,
impairment and classification emerged as central
developmental variables rather than administrative
considerations. Performance potential in wheelchair
tennis is strongly shaped by factors such as trunk
stability, limb function, balance, reach, propulsion
efficiency, and chair configuration. Classification
research indicates that these impairment‑related
characteristics influence both the rate and nature
of skill acquisition, creating distinct developmental
trajectories
(Tweedy
&
Vanlandewijck,
2011;
Beckman et al., 2017). When classification is treated
procedurally rather than developmentally, talent
systems risk inappropriate training loads, unrealistic
expectations, and misinterpretation of long‑term
potential. Injury‑focused research further highlights
interactions
between
impairment
profile
repetitive propulsion demands, reinforcing the need
for classification‑informed development and load
management (Mayrhuber et al., 2022).
Third, the synthesis highlighted that skill acquisition
in wheelchair tennis is governed by a fundamentally
different athlete–chair–environment system than
that
assumed
in
non-disabled
tennis
models.
Ecological dynamics research demonstrates that skill
emerges through continuous interaction between
the athlete, the wheelchair, task demands, and the
environment (Davids et al., 2013; Renshaw et al.,
2019). Match‑play analyses further indicate that
wheelchair tennis involves intermittent high‑intensity
propulsion,
frequent
directional
changes,
substantial upper‑limb loading, with considerable
variability across playing levels (Williamson et
al., 2024). In parallel, assessment research reveals
inconsistency in physical fitness testing, reflecting
limited standardisation within development systems
(Leale et al., 2025). Finally, social, psychological,
and environmental factors were found to exert a
disproportionate influence on talent development
in wheelchair tennis. Disability sport research
consistently identifies barriers including equipment
cost, transport limitations, facility accessibility, and
reliance on family or caregiver support (Martin, 2017;
Ruffault et al., 2017). These constraints shape both
entry into the sport and long‑term engagement, with
implications for progression and retention across
talent pathways. Collectively, these findings highlight
not isolated weaknesses but interconnected tensions
between pathway structure, coaching pedagogy, and
athlete experience, which are examined in greater
depth in the following discussion.
Discussion
Limitations of non-disabled talent models: early
specialisation and performance markers
The findings reinforce longstanding critiques of nondisabled talent development models, particularly
their reliance on early specialisation, uninterrupted
training trajectories, and early performance indicators
(Bailey et al., 2010; Côté & Vierimaa, 2014). Such
assumptions are poorly aligned with wheelchair
tennis, where athletes frequently enter the sport
later and progress through non‑linear developmental
pathways
shaped
by
rehabilitation,
functional
adaptation, and psychosocial transition (Dehghansai
et al., 2017). Consequently, early performance
markers—such as junior rankings or early technical
proficiency—risk excluding athletes with substantial
long‑term potential when performance reflects stages
of adaptation rather than underlying capability
(Vaeyens et al., 2008; Baker et al., 2012). Non-disabled
models further under‑recognise impairment‑related
constraints that shape skill acquisition in wheelchair
tennis, including trunk stability, limb function,
and propulsion mechanics (Fliess‑Douer et al.,
2012; Tweedy & Vanlandewijck, 2011). When these
factors are overlooked, developmental delays may
be misinterpreted as indicators of low potential,
particularly within systems that prioritise early
selection and deselection. Taken together, these
dynamics
indicate
that
performance‑centric,
early‑selection
models
are
ill‑suited
to
the
developmental realities of wheelchair tennis.
Structural
inequality
psychosocial
assumptions in pathway design
Beyond developmental assumptions, non-disabled
talent models implicitly presume equitable access
to coaching, facilities, equipment, and competitive
opportunities. In wheelchair tennis, development
is strongly shaped by structural constraints such as
equipment cost, transport limitations, and restricted
access to local training and competition environments
(Martin,
2017;
Brittain,
2016).
These
barriers
introduce variability in training continuity that is
largely absent from non-disabled pathways, rendering
early performance benchmarks inequitable. Structural
constraints
interact
closely
with
psychosocial
processes that influence sustained engagement
long‑term
development.
Qualitative
mixed‑methods research highlights the importance
of identity reconstruction, perceived competence,
autonomy, and social support in shaping wheelchair
tennis
participation
(Richardson
et
al.,
2010;
Papathomas et al., 2015). Sustained engagement is
therefore influenced less by early competitive success
and more by meaningful progression opportunities
and supportive environments.
The role of impairment, classification, and skill
acquisition
Impairment and classification emerged as foundational
developmental variables rather than administrative
considerations. Functional characteristics such as
trunk control, limb function, balance, reach, and
propulsion efficiency profoundly influence technical
execution, tactical options, and physical capacity
(Tweedy & Vanlandewijck, 2011; Fliess‑Douer et
al., 2012). Classification research demonstrates that
these characteristics shape distinct developmental
trajectories, influencing both the rate and nature
of skill acquisition (Beckman et al., 2017). When
classification is treated procedurally rather than
developmentally, talent systems risk inappropriate
training
expectations
misinterpretation
of
long‑term potential (Reina et al., 2020). Ecological
dynamics
provides
a
coherent
framework
for
understanding these demands, emphasising that skill
in wheelchair tennis emerges through continuous
interaction between the athlete, the wheelchair, and
the environment (Davids et al., 2013; Renshaw et al.,
2019). Unlike non-disabled tennis, propulsion, braking,
rotation, and stroke production are tightly coupled
actions that reshape perceptual–motor demands
and tactical possibilities (Fliess‑Douer et al., 2012).
Match‑play analyses further demonstrate the high
physical demands of wheelchair tennis (Williamson et
al., 2024), while inconsistencies in fitness assessment
reflect limited standardisation within development
systems (Leale et al., 2025).
Social and structural barriers
Social and structural barriers play a central role
in
shaping
talent
development
in
wheelchair
tennis, influencing both entry into the sport and
long‑term progression. Athletes with disabilities
commonly face constraints such as the high cost
of specialised sports wheelchairs, limited facility
accessibility, and reliance on family or caregivers
for transport and logistical support (Martin, 2017).
These barriers reflect wider structural inequalities
that restrict access to organised sport rather than
individual circumstances alone. Research in disability
studies shows that access to adaptive equipment,
transport, and inclusive environments is uneven,
disproportionately
disadvantaging
athletes
from
lower socioeconomic backgrounds or rural locations
(Shakespeare,
2014;
Brittain,
2016).
Regional
variation in coaching expertise, training groups, and
competitive opportunities further compounds these
inequities. Psychosocial factors interact closely with
these constraints, with social support identified as
a key determinant of sustained participation and
motivation in disability sport (Ruffault et al., 2017).
Where such support is limited, athletes may struggle
to maintain training consistency or develop a stable
athletic identity, particularly as entry into wheelchair
tennis often coincides with significant life transitions
such as adjustment to impairment and changes in
self‑concept (Martin, 2017). Collectively, these factors
mean that talent pathways risk overlooking athletes
with high potential due to contextual barriers rather
than capability.
Proposals – Drills – Exercises
Evidence from individual sports indicates that
misalignment between theory and applied coaching
practice is common, even in high‑performance
contexts (Mounter et al., 2025). In wheelchair tennis,
this gap is compounded by impairment‑specific
constraints,
equipment
demands,
variable
entry points. Accordingly, the following proposals
emphasise representative, adaptable, athlete‑centred
practice designs aligned with the athlete–chair–
environment system and the developmental realities
identified in this paper. Grounded in ecological
dynamics and disability sport research, these practices
prioritise perception–action coupling, functional
exploration, and the emergence of individualised
performance solutions rather than prescriptive
technique replication (Farrow & Robertson, 2017;
Williams & Hodges, 2005). Evidence also suggests
that manipulating task constraints and equipment
interaction
can
enhance
movement
efficiency,
decision‑making, and self‑efficacy (Sindall, 2016), and
coach‑education resources recommend prioritising
chair control, confidence, and adaptable movement
solutions over early outcome‑driven specialisation
(ITF, 2019; Schrijver, 2021).
At early and intermediate stages, a central priority
is coordinating propulsion with stroke production.
Dynamic propulsion–hit sequences (propel–brake–
rotate–hit) reflect the tightly coupled demands
of match play and support refinement of timing
between braking, rotation, and stroke preparation
(Fliess‑Douer et al., 2012). Task complexity can be
increased via lateral movement, variable propulsion
distances, or unpredictable feeds to elevate perceptual
and motor demands. Perceptual attunement can be
developed through variable bounce‑reading drills in
which ball speed, trajectory, and spin are manipulated
to promote earlier cue detection and rapid chair
orientation adjustments (Reina et al., 2020). Tactical
adaptability can be promoted through constraint‑led
point scenarios that encourage exploration rather
than replication, using manipulations such as reduced
court width, limits on propulsion pushes, or opponent
positioning (Davids et al., 2013; MacNamara &
Collins, 2015). To support functional reach and stroke
control—particularly where trunk function is limited—
stability‑under‑load hitting tasks using resistance
bands can develop stabilisation strategies relevant to
powerful and accurate strokes (Burkett, 2010).
Given the sport’s repetitive upper‑limb loading,
propulsion efficiency should be developed alongside
injury‑mitigation
principles.
Propulsion
rhythm
interval drills that combine propulsion, braking
control, and immediate transition into hitting
actions can support movement economy and braking
precision, consistent with propulsion research and
injury‑prevention priorities (Van der Woude et al.,
2001). Progressive load management and monitoring
remain important in this context, given reduced
recovery capacity and cumulative loading risk in
spinal cord injury populations (Jacobs et al., 2016).
Decision‑making under pressure can be developed via
rapid decision‑carousel drills requiring consecutive
responses to multi‑zone feeds, enhancing perceptual
speed and cognitive load management under time
constraints.
Finally,
structured
exploration
of
equipment adaptation should be embedded within
development.
Chair‑configuration
trials
(e.g.,
seat height, camber, strapping) can help athletes
and coaches understand how equipment affects
propulsion, stability, and stroke production; objective
comparisons (e.g., timed propulsion, turning‑radius
tasks,
stroke‑speed
assessments)
can
support
informed adjustments (Burkett, 2010). In parallel,
evidence‑based strength and conditioning should
prioritise shoulder health, trunk stability, propulsion
efficiency, and injury prevention given the repetitive
loading demands of racket‑assisted propulsion and
high‑intensity court movement (Roetert et al., 2023;
Mayrhuber et al., 2022).
What needs to change in wheelchair tennis talent
development?
While the preceding drills and practice examples
illustrate how theory‑informed coaching can be
enacted at the applied level, their effectiveness
ultimately depends on the broader talent pathway
structures within which they are embedded. For the
ambition articulated in the title—rethinking talent
pathways toward inclusive and evidence‑informed
development
models—to
be
realised,
several
interconnected changes are required at structural,
pedagogical, and cultural levels within wheelchair
tennis. The literature reviewed in this paper indicates
that current systems are not simply under‑resourced or
inconsistently applied, but are frequently misaligned
with the developmental realities of wheelchair tennis
athletes. Addressing this misalignment requires
moving beyond superficial adaptation of non-disabled
models toward a more fundamental reconfiguration of
how talent is identified, supported, and evaluated.
First, talent identification processes must shift away
from early, performance‑based selection models toward
approaches that recognise late entry and non‑linear
development as normative rather than exceptional.
In wheelchair tennis, early performance is strongly
influenced by factors such as stage of impairment
adaptation,
access
to
appropriate
equipment,
and prior sporting experience, all of which vary
substantially across athletes. Development systems
that prioritise early competitive success therefore risk
excluding individuals with high long‑term potential
whose performance is constrained by transitional or
contextual factors. More inclusive pathways would
emphasise broad engagement, extended sampling
opportunities, and ongoing re‑entry points rather
than narrow early selection thresholds. Second,
classification and impairment profiles need to be
integrated meaningfully into development planning
rather than treated as administrative necessities.
The reviewed evidence highlights that functional
characteristics such as trunk stability, limb function,
and propulsion capacity shape not only performance
outcomes but also the rate and nature of skill
acquisition. Talent pathways that fail to account for
these factors risk misinterpreting developmental
trajectories and applying inappropriate training
expectations.
Embedding
classification‑informed
frameworks within talent systems would allow
for more realistic benchmarking, individualised
progression timelines, and fairer evaluation of athlete
potential.
Third, coaching practice within wheelchair tennis
must move away from the direct transplantation of
non-disabled technical models toward pedagogies
grounded in the athlete–chair–environment system.
Ecological dynamics research indicates that effective
skill acquisition in wheelchair tennis depends on
exploration,
adaptability,
perception–action
coupling rather than replication of idealised techniques.
For this shift to occur, coach education programmes
need to prioritise disability‑specific knowledge,
constraint‑led learning principles, and equipment
literacy. Without such changes, even well‑intentioned
development initiatives risk reinforcing mismatches
between coaching practice and performance demands.
Fourth, greater recognition of social and structural
constraints is required within talent pathway
design. Access to specialised wheelchairs, transport,
facilities, and qualified coaching remains uneven,
with significant implications for training consistency
and long‑term engagement. Inclusive development
models must therefore extend beyond on‑court
practices to incorporate financial support mechanisms,
regional development hubs, and partnerships with
rehabilitation and community organisations. Treating
these factors as peripheral rather than central to
development perpetuates inequities and narrows the
effective talent pool.
Finally, evaluation of success within wheelchair tennis
pathways must be broadened beyond short‑term
performance
outcomes.
Retention,
progression,
skill
adaptability,
sustained
engagement
are
equally
important
indicators
of
pathway
effectiveness, particularly in a sport characterised by
diverse entry points and developmental timelines.
Evidence‑informed
systems
would
value
these
outcomes explicitly, aligning performance objectives
with ethical commitments to opportunity and
inclusion. Taken together, these changes suggest
that rethinking talent pathways in wheelchair tennis
requires more than incremental adjustment. It
demands a coordinated shift in assumptions about
who talent is, how it develops, and what conditions
are necessary for it to flourish. Only through
such changes can wheelchair tennis move toward
development models that are genuinely inclusive,
evidence‑informed, and aligned with the complex
realities of the sport.
Conclusions
Wheelchair tennis requires a shift toward inclusive,
evidence‑informed talent pathways that reflect
the sport’s unique performance demands and
developmental
realities.
Models
adapted
from
non-disabled tennis continue to overlook the
complex interplay between impairment, equipment,
environment, and psychosocial factors that shape
both participation and performance (Shakespeare,
2014; Collins  & Winter, 2019). Through a synthesis
of performance science, disability sport research,
and talent development theory, this paper has
demonstrated that such omissions are not peripheral,
but central to the limitations of current pathway
design.
By
integrating
ecological
principles,
classification‑specific considerations, and applied
coaching practices, this paper has illustrated how
talent development systems can better accommodate
late entry, non‑linear progression, and diverse
functional profiles. The proposed drills and practice
designs demonstrate how theory‑informed coaching
can be operationalised in ways that support
adaptable skill acquisition, sustained engagement,
and long‑term athlete development. At a pathway
level, the findings highlight the need to move beyond
narrow performance benchmarks toward models
that value progression, retention, and opportunity
alongside competitive outcomes. This shift is not
only a performance imperative, but also an ethical
one. Developing pathways that recognise diversity
in impairment, entry points, and support contexts is
essential for ensuring equitable access to high‑quality
development opportunities within wheelchair tennis.
Collectively, evidence from applied performance
research,
injury
science,
inclusion‑focused
studies reinforces the need for wheelchair‑specific,
classification‑informed, and context‑sensitive talent
pathways that extend beyond superficial adaptations
of non-disabled models (Roetert et al., 2023;
Montesano et al., 2022; Sindall, 2016; Williamson
et al., 2024; Leale et al., 2025; Richardson et al.,
2010). Only through such an integrated approach can
wheelchair tennis create development systems that
are both effective and just, supporting diverse forms
of talent to flourish across the sport’s competitive
lifespan.
