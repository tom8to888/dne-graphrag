Abstract
Wheelchair tennis performance emerges from tightly coupled propulsion, chair orientation, stroke
production, and tactical decision-making under temporal constraint. However, applied coaching
environments frequently rely on decontextualised technical repetition that may weaken perception–
action coupling and reduce transfer to competition. This applied technical note integrates ecological
dynamics (Davids et al., 2013), representative learning design (Pinder et al., 2011), and the constraints-led
approach (Renshaw et al., 2019) to provide structured guidance for designing representative wheelchair
tennis practice drills. Five key performance demands are identified as essential to representativeness:
mobility–stroke integration, tactical utilisation of the two-bounce rule, serve and return specificity,
contextual shot selection, and fatigue-representative performance (Sindall et al., 2013; Williamson
et al., 2024). Stage-specific recommendations are presented for beginner, intermediate, and elite
contexts, aligned with inclusive and non-linear development pathways (Mounter, 2026). Practical
evaluation indicators and strength and conditioning considerations are provided to support sustainable,
transferable, and classification-aware skill acquisition.
Key words: wheelchair tennis; representative learning design; ecological dynamics; constraints-led approach.

Representative learning design (RLD)
Representative
learning

must preserve the key informational variables and
action possibilities that define the competitive
environment (Pinder et al., 2011). Importantly,
representativeness does not require full replication
of match complexity at all times. Rather, it requires
retention of those informational constraints that
shape perception, decision-making, and coordination
in performance contexts. RLD can be operationalised
in

First,

preserve the uncertainty inherent in competition.
Second, embedding task consequences through
scoring and tactical objectives increases decision
salience. For example, awarding bonus points for
returns landing beyond the service line aligns drill
incentives with match-relevant outcomes. Preserving
realistic
mobility

ensures
propulsion

(Broadbent et al., 2015; Pinder et al., 2011). These
principles are especially salient in wheelchair tennis

Methods and procedure
This paper is based on a narrative synthesis of
literature from ecological dynamics (Davids et al.,
2013), representative learning design (Pinder et al.,
2011), the constraints-led approach (Renshaw et al.,
2019), wheelchair tennis match analysis (Sindall et al.,
2013; Williamson et al., 2024), classification research
(Tweedy & Vanlandewijck, 2011), and wheelchair
tennis strength and conditioning practice (Roetert
et al., 2024). The aim was to integrate theoretical
foundations with applied coaching realities to produce
implementable design principles.
Synthesis of key performance domains
Five interconnected domains underpin representative
wheelchair tennis practice design. First, mobility–
stroke integration requires coordinated propulsion,
braking, chair orientation, and racquet acceleration.
Second, the two-bounce rule shapes spacing and
defensive problem-solving. Third, serve and return
exchanges
require
preservation
of
stationary
acceleration
constraints
and
early
perceptual
attunement.
Fourth,
contextual
shot
selection
depends on interacting informational constraints
including time, chair angle, opponent positioning,
and score. Fifth, repeated high-intensity propulsion
efforts introduce fatigue-related changes influencing
coordination and injury risk. These domains provide
the structural foundation for representative drill
design.

Constraints-led approach (CLA)
The constraints-led approach (CLA) provides a
practical framework for implementing ecological
dynamics and RLD principles in coaching contexts
(Renshaw et al., 2019). CLA proposes that behaviour
emerges from the interaction of task, environmental,
and individual constraints, and that manipulating
these constraints can shape coordination without
prescribing explicit technical solutions. In wheelchair
tennis, task constraints include court dimensions,
scoring systems, target zones, serve and return
conditions, bounce allowances, and rally-length
requirements,
while
environmental
constraints
may involve surface characteristics, lighting, or
competitive noise. Individual constraints encompass
strength, trunk stability, range of motion, injury
history, classification profile, and chair configuration.
The interaction among these factors shapes the
coordination patterns that emerge in practice.
CLA is particularly valuable in wheelchair tennis
given the heterogeneity of functional ability across
athletes. Impairment profile influences trunk control,
propulsion efficiency, and stroke mechanics (Tweedy &
Vanlandewijck, 2011), while chair configuration—such
as camber angle, seat height, and strapping—further
shapes movement affordances (Roetert et al., 2024).
Uniform drill prescriptions may therefore impose
inequitable challenge. Through intentional constraint
manipulation, coaches can individualise difficulty
while preserving tactical meaning. For example,
adjusting bounce allowance can scale defensive
challenge without removing decision-making, and

Wheelchair tennis involves repeated high-intensity
propulsion efforts interspersed with brief recovery.
Upper-body fatigue influences propulsion efficiency,
braking control, and stroke stability, with implications
for performance and injury risk (Mayrhuber et al.,
2022). Physiological demands vary by rally length and
classification (Williamson et al., 2024), supporting
context-sensitive
conditioning.
Representative

while maintaining controlled work-to-rest ratios.
However, representativeness does not equate to
excessive volume; structured recovery and load

Two-bounce rule and spacing decisions
The two-bounce rule introduces a distinctive tactical
affordance that expands defensive possibilities by
increasing temporal margin and enabling repositioning
(Reina et al., 2021). Players may intentionally use
the second bounce to regain central coverage or
neutralise
pace.
Consequently,
representative
practice should not consistently demand first-bounce
contact. Overemphasising immediate contact may
limit defensive adaptability under pressure. Instead,
training should reflect the dual tactical reality of
wheelchair tennis: first-bounce aggression when space
permits and deliberate second-bounce management
when
constrained.
Excluding
either
affordance
reduces ecological validity.
Serve and return specificity
Serve and return phases are especially influential due
to constrained acceleration from a stationary base.
Wheelchair athletes must generate court coverage
through upper-limb propulsion from rest, increasing
the importance of early cue pickup and efficient first
pushes. Return effectiveness depends on attunement
to toss motion, racquet kinematics, and trajectory.
Early exchanges shape tactical and physical demands
(Williamson et al., 2024), reinforcing the need to
preserve serve–return dynamics in practice. Initiating
rallies from predictable mid-court feeds removes
defining informational constraints. Representative
serve and return practice should therefore include
realistic serve speeds, directional uncertainty, and
scoring consequences aligned with match objectives.

Proposal – Drills – Exercises
Effective representative practice design in wheelchair
tennis begins with clarifying the point-deciding
performance problems and the interacting constraints
that shape functional solutions in competition.
Drill construction should therefore be grounded in
the ecological demands of match play rather than
isolated technical rehearsal. Match-play analyses,
classification research, and applied strength and
conditioning literature highlight five interconnected
domains that must be preserved within training:
mobility–stroke integration, tactical implications
of the two-bounce rule, serve and return specificity,
contextual shot selection, and fatigue-representative
performance (Sindall et al., 2013; Reina et al., 2021;
Williamson et al., 2024; Mayrhuber et al., 2022).

Beginner stage: exploration, confidence, and
coupled coordination.
Entry into wheelchair tennis is frequently nonlinear and often occurs following rehabilitation or
significant life transition (Mounter, 2026). At this
stage, performance variability reflects adaptation
to impairment, chair-handling acquisition, and
psychosocial adjustment rather than stable technical
ceiling. Consequently, practice design must prioritise
confidence, autonomy, and integrated coordination
rather than early performance benchmarking. A
central design principle at this stage is coupling chair
movement and stroke production from the outset.
Foundational drills should incorporate propel–brake–
rotate–hit sequences even at low intensity.
For example, in a half-court crosscourt lane drill,
players may be required to execute at least one
propulsion adjustment and controlled braking action
before contact. Scoring can reward balls landing beyond
the service line, with additional points awarded if the
athlete returns to a designated “home base” position
prior to the next feed. This embeds both directional
intent and recovery awareness while preserving
manageable load. The two-bounce rule should be
legitimised early as a tactical affordance rather than
framed as compensatory failure. A defensive reset game
may involve variable feeds across full baseline width,
where points are awarded for deep, high-trajectory
balls that successfully regain time and positioning.
Progressions may include transitioning from coachcontrolled feeds to live opponent exchanges, thereby
increasing informational uncertainty. Small-sided
games are particularly effective in this stage. Reduced
court width lowers propulsion load while preserving
decision-making. Short bouts (30–60 seconds) help
manage upper-limb loading, consistent with injury
prevention considerations highlighted by Mayrhuber
et al. (2022). Strength and conditioning integration
at this level should emphasise braking mechanics
instruction and posterior chain activation, aligning
with recommendations from Roetert et al. (2024).
Importantly, traditional blocked drills that isolate
technique without movement risk reproducing the
theory–practice mismatch described by Mounter et al.
(2025). Drills may appear structured yet fail to represent
match demands. Ensuring that even beginner tasks
embed decision-making and recovery requirements
helps avoid this early fragmentation. Figure 2
illustrates an example representative beginner drill,

Conclusions
Representative wheelchair tennis practice design
requires preservation of the informational cues,
mobility–stroke
couplings,
temporal
pressures,
and tactical affordances that define competition.
Ecological dynamics, representative learning design,
and the constraints-led approach provide a coherent
theoretical foundation for aligning daily practice with
performance demands. Stage-specific implementation
is essential. Beginners require exploratory yet coupled
tasks that legitimise second-bounce solutions and
build confidence. Intermediate athletes benefit from
constraint-gated pattern development and integrated
conditioning. Elite athletes require opponent-specific,
fatigue-representative, pressure-informed practice
while maintaining sustainable load management.
By embedding representativeness within inclusive,
classification-aware
pathways
and
integrating
strength and conditioning considerations, coaches
can enhance adaptability, transfer, and long-term
athlete development. Bridging theory and practice
in this manner supports not only performance
enhancement but sustainable engagement across the
athlete lifespan.
