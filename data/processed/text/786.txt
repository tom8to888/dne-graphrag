Early Development: Limited Structure,
Minimal Evidence
In the late 1980s and early 1990s, wheelchair tennis
was characterised by low participation numbers,
limited competition structures, and minimal scientific
understanding. Athletes such as four-time Paralympian
Jayant Mistry competed at the inaugural Barcelona
1992 Games (Figure 1) and went on to win a Wimbledon
wheelchair doubles title in 2005. They operated in an
environment with few tournaments, limited funding,
and no formal performance system. At this stage,
training was largely unstructured and based on general
activity rather than sport-specific preparation. In
1993, the International Wheelchair Tennis Foundation
(IWTF) organised its first Wheelchair Tennis Coaches
Conference in Austria, identifying key themes that
national coaches could disseminate within their
respective countries, marking an early step towards

Abstract
Over the past three decades, GB wheelchair tennis has evolved from a largely informal participation
sport into a highly professional, performance-driven discipline underpinned by applied sports science.
This transformation has been driven by sustained collaboration between athletes, practitioners, and
academic institutions, enabling the development of evidence-based approaches to training, equipment
design, and competition preparation. Early stages were characterised by limited infrastructure,
minimal scientific understanding, and unstructured training. However, targeted research in physiology,
biomechanics, and performance analysis established the foundation for structured athlete development.
Advances in field- and laboratory-based testing have enabled practitioners to identify key determinants
of performance, including aerobic and anaerobic capacity, mobility efficiency, and technical proficiency.
Research has highlighted the importance of movement efficiency and the athlete-wheelchair interface,
with optimised chair configuration and propulsion mechanics enhancing performance and reducing
injury risk. Interdisciplinary support, including nutrition, recovery, and thermal management strategies,
has further improved athlete preparation. Collectively, the GB model demonstrates how integrated
sports science can transform Paralympic sport, contributing to sustained international success and
providing a framework for future innovation.
Key words: athlete-equipment interaction; performance analysis; thermoregulation; wheelchair mobility.

Establishing an Evidence Base for
Performance
The late 1990s and early 2000s marked the beginning
of systematic research into wheelchair tennis
performance, particularly following Sydney 2000
Paralympic Games. Prior to this period, competitive
success had been concentrated among a small number
of nations, with only five countries (The Netherlands,
USA, Australia, Germany and France), winning
medals across the previous three Paralympic Games
(Bunting, 2001). This concentration highlighted both
the limited global performance depth and the need
for a more robust, evidence-based approach to athlete
development.
University’s
in
UK,
notably
Manchester
Metropolitan
University
and
Loughborough
University, played a pivotal role in advancing applied
research in the sport. These institutions contributed
to the development of athlete testing and monitoring
methodologies, introducing both field-based and
laboratory protocols (Figure 2a and 2b) to assess
aerobic and anaerobic capacity, mobility performance,
and sport-specific fitness (Goosey-Tolfrey & Leicht,
2013). These approaches formed the foundation
for more structured training programmes and, in
collaboration with individual and national coaches,
enabled the identification of key performance
determinants across the men’s, women’s, and quad
divisions. Training environments began to evolve, with
performance camps and training weekends becoming
increasingly interdisciplinary, integrating coaching,
sport science, and medical support. This marked a
transition towards more focused, performance-based
programmes, including systematic player screening
and profiling practices.
Importantly, research demonstrated that wheelchair
tennis performance is multifactorial, influenced not
only by physiological capacity but also by movement
efficiency and court mobility. Match-play analysis
revealed that higher-ranked players typically move
faster and cover greater distances while operating
at similar physiological loads to their lower-ranked
counterparts, highlighting efficiency rather than
effort as a key differentiator (Sindall et al., 2013).
Subsequent work further demonstrated that match
context, including score margin and competition
level, significantly influences the physical and
technical demands of play (Mason et al., 2020).
Collectively, this growing evidence base underpinned
the professionalisation of the sport and informed the
development of more targeted, sport-specific training
interventions.
The Athlete-Chair Interface: Ergonomics
and Biomechanics
A defining feature of wheelchair tennis is the interaction
between the athlete and their equipment. Research
into wheelchair configuration has demonstrated that
performance is dependent on the combined system
of athlete and chair, rather than in isolation (Mason
et al., 2013). Advances in ergonomics have shown
that variables such as seat position, wheel camber,
and hand-rim design directly influence propulsion
efficiency, mobility, and injury risk. In addition, the
presence of a racket introduces further biomechanical
constraints on propulsion, as athletes must generate
force asymmetrically while maintaining ball control.
Propelling with a racket has been shown to reduce
wheelchair velocity compared to propulsion without,
highlighting the additional technical and physical
demands imposed during tennis-specific movement
(Goosey-Tolfrey and Moss, 2005).
Early
qualitative
and
experimental
studies
highlighted how athletes perceive and adapt their
chair configurations to optimise performance (Mason
et al., 2010; Mason et al., 2013). Recent technological
developments have further enhanced this field. The
use of inertial measurement units (IMUs) now allows
detailed quantification of drag forces, propulsion
mechanics, and power output during tennis-specific
movement tasks (Rietveld et al., 2024). These
innovations enable more precise individualisation of
equipment and training interventions.

has been critical to performance development.

highlighted methodological limitations and the need
for higher-resolution tracking systems in wheelchair
sport (Sindall et al., 2013). More broadly, early wheelrotation logger approaches can be accurate at low
speeds but show meaningful error when speeds

define wheelchair tennis. In IMU-derived datasets,

high-speed propulsion (Rietveld et al., 2023). Applied

developments have strengthened the specificity of

individualise conditioning and mobility interventions.

Thermal Physiology and Environmental
Challenges
Wheelchair tennis is frequently played in challenging
environmental conditions, with major competitions
such as the Paralympic Games and Grand Slam
tournaments often held in hot and humid climates.
These conditions place significant thermoregulatory
demands on athletes particularly for athletes with
spinal cord injury (SCI), where reduced sweating and
vasomotor control below the lesion can constrain
heat dissipation and increase heat storage (Griggs
et al., 2015). In match settings, even under moderate
environmental conditions, wheelchair tennis can
elicit meaningful increases in core body temperature.
For example, during a 45 min singles match at
the British Open (WBGT ~18–20 °C), core body
temperature increased by ~0.6 ± 0.1 °C in players with
SCI compared to ~0.3 ± 0.1 °C in players without a
SCI, despite similar match intensity (Veltmeijer et al.,
2014). This greater rise may reflect reduced capacity
for heat dissipation during intermittent recovery
periods, leading to cumulative thermal strain in
athletes with a SCI.
Practitioner
Development
and
Embedded Sports Scientist Model
Alongside advances in physiology, biomechanics, and
performance analysis, a defining feature of the GB
wheelchair tennis system has been its approach to
practitioner development. This centred on embedded,
athlete-focused model of sports science delivery.
Rather than working as external consultants, sports
scientists were integrated into daily training and
competition environments. They worked closely
with athletes and coaches to co-develop applied
solutions. Importantly, GB wheelchair tennis was one
of the few Paralympic sports at the time to include
a dedicated physiologist with its own support team,
as demonstrated at the Athens 2004 Paralympic
Games. This early integration of sport science support
helped establish a more holistic and interdisciplinary
performance model.
The embedded approach played a key role in both
knowledge translation and practitioner development.
Early examples include Nik Diaper (Figure 3),
who gained initial experience as a master’s-level
physiologist at the Athens 2004 Paralympic Games.
This exposure contributed to his later leadership roles
within the English Institute of Sport (now UK Sports
Institute), where he helped shape interdisciplinary
support models across Paralympic programmes.
Similarly, John Lenton’s early applied work within
wheelchair tennis supported his progression to senior
roles within British Cycling’s Paralympic programme.
This highlights the transferability of expertise
developed within disability sport environments.
Central to this approach has been a philosophy of
co-production, where research questions emerge
directly from athlete and coach needs rather than
being imposed externally. This aligns with broader
perspectives in Paralympic sport science, which
emphasise the importance of “asking the right
questions” and developing context-specific solutions
in collaboration with athletes (Goosey-Tolfrey, 2010;
Paulson & Goosey-Tolfrey, 2017). Such an approach
has
enabled
rapid
iteration
between
research
and practice, ensuring that scientific insights are
both ecologically valid and directly applicable.
The embedded scientist model has also facilitated
integration
of
multidisciplinary
expertise,
allowing practitioners to link physiological testing,
biomechanical analysis, and performance tracking
within a coherent support framework. Importantly,
this model has extended beyond the UK, with similar
approaches now being adopted internationally as
other nations seek to replicate the success of British
Paralympic sport systems. Collectively, practitioner
development and embedded sports science delivery
have been key drivers of innovation in wheelchair
tennis, highlighting that performance gains are not
only the result of new technologies and methods, but
also of the environments and systems in which they
are developed and applied.

In the UK, this transition is evident across multiple
platforms. Wimbledon, broadcast by the BBC, has
progressively elevated wheelchair tennis by moving
matches onto show courts and into live broadcast slots,
exposing wider audiences to the speed, technical skill,
and tactical complexity of the sport. This increased
visibility has translated into growing audiences, with
viewing figures for wheelchair tennis at Wimbledon
rising as matches have been showcased alongside
able-bodied events, reinforcing its legitimacy as
high-performance sport. Complementing this, digital
innovations such as the BBC Red Button and live
streaming have expanded access beyond traditional
broadcast schedules, exemplified by coverage of
events such as Loughborough Lightning wheelchair
basketball. These platforms allow audiences to engage
with Para sport in real time, increasing both reach and
depth of engagement (LTA, 2022).
Early work from Webborn and colleagues (2010)
demonstrated that in a controlled hot environment
(32 °C; 50% RH), precooling and cooling during
exercise (with an ice vest) significantly attenuated
increases in core temperature (37.3 ± 0.3 °C control vs
36.5 ± 0.6 °C precooling and 37.0 ± 0.5 °C per-cooling;
P < 0.01), alongside reductions in perceived exertion
(Webborn et al., 2005). Subsequent work confirmed
the performance relevance of these interventions,
with cooling strategies improving tolerance to
intermittent high-intensity exercise in the heat. Time
to exhaustion was extended (e.g., ~52.8 ± 5.8 min
precooling vs ~47.2 ± 10.8 min control), demonstrating
that reducing thermal strain can preserve repeatedeffort capacity (Webborn et al., 2010).
From a mechanistic perspective, the effectiveness
of cooling strategies is strongly influenced by heat
transfer properties. Water has approximately 25-27
times greater thermal conductivity than air, meaning
water-based cooling methods can remove heat more
effectively when sufficient surface area contact is
achieved (Griggs et al., 2015). For example, cold-water
immersion (~10 °C for ~20 min) can remove ~100150 W of heat, although such approaches are often
impractical during match play and may interfere with
hand function in wheelchair sports (Griggs et al.,
2015). In contrast, commonly used strategies such
as wet towels or brief water dousing may provide
only localised cooling with limited impact on core
temperature, particularly in humid conditions where
evaporative cooling is restricted (Figure 4). Evidence
suggests such approaches do not consistently improve
performance or perceptual responses in SCI athletes
when used in isolation (Griggs et al., 2015). Practically,
this highlights the need for a layered approach to
thermal management. Combining internal cooling
(e.g., ice slurry ingestion), targeted external cooling
(e.g., cooling garments), and structured hydration
strategies is likely to be more effective than single
interventions alone. In wheelchair tennis, ice slurry
ingestion (~6.8 g·kg⁻¹ total dose) has been shown to
reduce gastrointestinal temperature and improve
thermal sensation during exercise in the heat
(Stephenson et al., 2022). Hydration also requires
careful planning, as interventions that improve
thermal comfort may reduce voluntary fluid intake.
For instance, cooling garments have been shown to
reduce ad libitum fluid consumption despite ongoing
sweat
losses
(~200–1300 mL),
emphasising
need for structured hydration strategies rather than
reliance on thirst alone (Goosey-Tolfrey et al., 2008).

Conclusion: A Model of Integrated
Sports Science
The evolution of GB wheelchair tennis provides a
clear example of how sports science can transform
a sport. From early descriptive studies to advanced
biomechanical modelling and applied interventions,
research has informed every aspect of performance,
from equipment design to training, recovery, and
competition preparation. Crucially, this progress
has
been
driven
by
an
integrated
approach,
combining laboratory research, field-based testing,
and practitioner expertise alongside individual and
national coaching staff. The embedded sports scientist
model, alongside the development of practitioners
within applied environments, has been central to
this success, enabling rapid translation of research
into practice. As the sport continues to evolve, future
developments in data analytics, artificial intelligence,
and personalised performance modelling are likely to
further enhance athlete preparation. The success of
GB wheelchair tennis on the global stage reflects not
only athlete talent, but also the sustained impact of
science-informed practice, demonstrating the value
of interdisciplinary collaboration in Paralympic sport.
Importantly, this progression now extends beyond
the training environment. The growing alignment
between sports science, performance systems, and
media representation ensures that wheelchair tennis
is not only advancing in practice, but increasingly
communicated, evaluated and recognised as an elite
sport on the global stage.

The Paralympic Games have been particularly
influential in reshaping media narratives. Since
London 2012, UK broadcasters most notably Channel 4
have transformed the Paralympics into a mainstream
sporting spectacle, delivering unprecedented levels of
coverage and accessibility. For example, the Paris 2024
Paralympics reached nearly 20 million UK viewers and
generated billions of minutes of viewing across linear
and streaming platforms, highlighting both the scale
of audience engagement and the demand for Para
sport content. Importantly, research indicates that
this exposure has shifted how audiences perceive
disability sport. Viewers increasingly recognise
Paralympic competition as elite sport, valuing athletic
performance and competitive excellence rather than
solely personal narratives (Pullen et al. 2020).
Academic work within media and communication has
highlighted this shift as part of a broader transition
from “inspirational” framing to “sporting legitimacy.”
Paralympic broadcasting has contributed to the
normalisation of elite disabled athletes, positioning
them
within
high-performance
sport
discourse
and emphasising metrics such as speed, distance,
tactical execution, and medal success. This aligns
closely with developments in sports science, where
performance data, biomechanics, and match analytics
now underpin both preparation and commentary. As
a result, media coverage increasingly mirrors that
of non-disabled sport, incorporating performance
statistics, tactical breakdowns, and expert analysis
reinforcing the credibility and professionalism of
Para sport. In this way, media does not merely reflect
progress in wheelchair tennis; it actively reinforces it,
shaping how athletes are understood, evaluated, and
celebrated within the wider sporting landscape.
Key Messages for Coaches and Practitioners
•	 Performance
is
multidisciplinary:
Success
is underpinned by integrated support teams
combining physiology, biomechanics, nutrition,
and psychology.
•	 Movement efficiency is performance-critical:
Higher-ranked players move faster and more
effectively
without
increasing
physiological
strain, highlighting the importance of technical
mobility training.
•	 The athlete–chair interface is central: Optimal
wheelchair configuration (e.g. seat position,
camber, hand-rim) directly affects propulsion
efficiency, performance, and injury risk.
•	 Test, monitor, individualise: Reliable field and
laboratory testing enables tailored training
programmes and better understanding of athletespecific needs.
