Introduction
Wheelchair tennis (WCT) has grown rapidly, sharing
rules, court dimensions, and equipment with ablebodied tennis (ABT). The key distinction is the
“double bounce” rule, allowing two bounces before
return (ITF, 2012). Moreover, competing from a seated
position fundamentally changes the sport’s physical
and technical demands (Sánchez-Pay et al., 2015).

Abstract
Wheelchair tennis (WCT) shares rules with able-bodied tennis (ABT) but serving from a seated position
imposes unique biomechanical constraints. This study explored the kinematic factors influencing a WCT
serve, compared them with an ABT, and examined the impact of impairment severity. One elite ABT
player and three elite WCT players with varying functional capacities performed first serves. Threedimensional motion analysis captured upper-body, racket, ball, and wheelchair kinematics, and the six
fastest successful serves per participant were analysed descriptively. Ball speed and spin were lower in
WCT (61.3 - 81.9 mph; ~1403 rpm) than ABT (128.3 mph; 2767 rpm), decreasing with impairment severity.
WCT players exhibited lower toss heights, backward ball displacement, restricted thorax motion, and
compensatory strategies such as increased trunk twist and shoulder elevation. Shoulder internal rotation
and wrist flexion were the primary contributors to racket speed, with greater reliance on wrist motion
in WCT. Serve projection angles for the WCT players were positive due to lower impact height. Findings
highlight that while fundamental serve principles apply across tennis formats, impairment-specific
adaptations are essential. Coaches should tailor training to trunk mobility, upper-limb mechanics, and
wheelchair positioning to optimise performance and reduce injury risk for WCT players.

Achieving high racket speeds relies on coordinated
upper-body segmental motion within a “kinetic
chain,” enabling optimal racket position and velocity
within approximately two seconds (Elliott et al.,
1995). Lower-limb propulsion plays a critical role in
initiating this chain, enhancing racket displacement
and subsequent segmental actions (Elliott et al., 1986;
Bartlett et al., 1995; Girard et al., 2005; Reid et al.,
2007a).
In WCT, serve speeds are lower than ABT when
measured in competition-relevant research and radarbased studies, yet elite WCT players still generate
relatively high ball velocities despite no effective
lower-limb contribution (Mecheri et al., 2016;
Cavedon et al., 2014; Sánchez-Pay et al., 2021). For
example, elite wheelchair players achieve pre-impact
racket velocities of approximately 32 m/s compared
to 42 m/s in ABT (Reid et al., 2007b). This difference
is largely attributed to compensatory strategies
involving upper-body strength and coordination,
as the kinetic chain is truncated and trunk motion
restricted. Coaches often assume that contributors
identified in ABT research apply to WCT; however,
biomechanical adaptations such as increased reliance
on shoulder internal rotation and wrist extension are
evident (Reid et al., 2007b; Goosey-Tolfrey & Moss,
2005). Despite these adaptations, few studies have
examined how impairment severity influences serve
performance. Evidence suggests that players with less
severe impairments produce significantly higher serve
speeds (Cavedon et al., 2014), and shoulder kinetics
vary according to spinal cord injury level (Reid et al.,
2007b). Furthermore, upper-body power, measured
through medicine-ball throws, strongly predicts
serve velocity (r ~ 0.92) and accuracy, highlighting its
critical role in compensating for reduced trunk and
leg involvement (Sánchez-Pay et al., 2021; Alim et al.,
2023).
Few studies have systematically identified the factors
that contribute to a powerful serve in wheelchair
tennis or examined how these vary with impairment
severity. Understanding these factors is crucial
for coaches, as it enables them to tailor training
programs to individual functional capacities, optimise
performance, and reduce injury risk. Therefore, this
study aims to investigate the key kinematic factors
that characterise a fast and effective wheelchair tennis
serve; compare these with those observed in an elite
ABT player and explore how differences in functional
capacity influence serve technique and performance.

Discussion
This study provides an initial descriptive analysis of
kinematic parameters influencing a 1st serve in WCT.
The six fastest serves from three WCT players with
varying impairments were examined, alongside an AB
player for comparison. As expected, ball speed and
spin in WCT were lower than ABT and decreased with
impairment severity. The AB player’s serve velocities
(average 128.3 ± 8.8 mph) aligned with previous
research (Chow et al., 2003; Fleisig et al., 2003).
WCT speeds ranged from 81.9 ± 11.4 to 61.5 ± 3.9
mph, similar to Sanchez-Pay et al., (2021). Spin rates
averaged 2767 rpm for AB and ~1403 rpm for WCT,
with the least functional players hitting nearly flat
serves. These findings agree with previous AB studies
(Goodwill et al., 2006; Kelley et al., 2008). Overall,
seated position reduced post-impact ball linear and
angular velocity despite maximal effort.
The acceleration phase represented the final 10% of
the serve for all players, except WCT2 who used a
considerably shorter ball toss duration. AB player toss
characteristics matched previous findings for a first
serve (Chow et al. 2003; Reid et al. 2011). For AB, the
ball released at 1.65 m and 0.29 m right of the left foot,
moving forward towards the baseline while in WCT
release height ranged 0.49 - 0.74 m and was lower
with greater impairment. Seated posture, wheelchair
design/configuration, and anthropometrics likely
explain observed variability. Notably, WCT tosses
moved backwards from release to impact, opposite
to AB, likely due to limited thorax flexion and static
wheelchair position. WCT1 and WCT2 showed similar

The results also suggest that impairment severity
influences the movement strategies used during
the serve. WCT players with greater functional
limitations appear to compensate through increased
shoulder elevation and altered racket trajectories.
Coaches should avoid applying a single technical
model across all WCT players and instead adopt an
individualised approach that reflects each player's
functional capacity and impairment profile. Finally,
wheelchair configuration may substantially influence
serve mechanics by affecting trunk movement, ball
toss characteristics and impact position. Coaches and
equipment specialists should work collaboratively to
optimise wheelchair setup, including seating position,
backrest height and strapping, to balance stability
with functional range of motion during serving.
Collectively, these findings support a player-centred
approach to WCT serve development, where technical
instruction, physical preparation and wheelchair
configuration are tailored to the athlete's specific
functional abilities to maximise performance while
minimising injury risk.
Summary
Most factors influencing ABT serve also apply to WCT,
though impairment type alters thorax and shoulder
mechanics, ball toss, and joint contributions. WCT
players compensate for limited trunk motion through
greater shoulder and wrist movement. Wheelchair
configuration and toss technique are critical and
warrant further study. Despite a small sample, this
work provides a foundation insight into WCT serve
kinematics for coaches. Future work should expand on
this work across divisions and impairment types and
sexes, supporting evidence-based coaching strategies.

PRACTICAL APPLICATIONS
The findings of this study have several practical
implications for WCT coaches, athletes, and support
practitioners. While the fundamental biomechanical
principles underpinning serve performance appear
similar to those in AB tennis, the seated position
and varying levels of functional impairment require
individualised technical and physical interventions.
1.	Coaches should place particular emphasis on
optimising ball toss mechanics. The lower release
height and backward ball displacement observed
in WCT highlight the importance of developing
a consistent toss that maximises impact height
while remaining achievable within the athlete’s
functional capabilities. Individual adaptations
may be required according to trunk control, upperlimb function and wheelchair configuration.
2.	Our observations confirm that trunk function
appears to play a critical role in tennis serve
performance,
as
also
indicated
in
recent
work by Hakariya et al., (2026). WCT players
with greater thorax mobility achieved larger
racket displacements and higher serve speeds.
Consequently,
training
programmes
should
aim to maximise available trunk rotation, side
flexion, stability and control, where appropriate
and safe. This may include rotational and sideflexion focused trunk exercises, as well as
overhead trunk stability work where possible,
to enhance the contribution of the trunk within
the wider biomechanical chain. For athletes with
impaired trunk function, the emphasis should be
on maximising the function they have available,
while using technical and physical strategies
that support effective shoulder and upper-limb
contribution. Throwing-based exercises, such as
a Nerf Vortex, tennis ball or small light medicine
ball, may also provide a practical way to develop
upper-limb speed and power relevant to the serve.
3.	Shoulder internal rotation and wrist flexion were
major contributors to racket head velocity in all
participants, with WCT players demonstrating an
even greater reliance on wrist motion. Strength
and conditioning programmes should therefore
target the shoulder rotators, scapular stabilisers
and forearm musculature, while also being
aware of gradually building up serve volume and
intensity to manage injury risk.
