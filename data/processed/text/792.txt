Introduction
Wheelchair tennis has grown from a rehabilitation
activity into one of the world's most established
Paralympic sports. Participation continues to increase
through community programmes, schools, clubs and
international competition, creating opportunities for
players of all ages and abilities to engage with the sport
(Paralympic, 2026). As participation grows, so too does
the need for coaches who are confident introducing
beginners to wheelchair tennis, yet it may represent
unfamiliar territory for some. Experienced tennis
coaches may have limited knowledge of wheelchair
mobility, equipment or adapting practice for players
with different physical abilities. Equally, coaches
entering disability sport from other backgrounds may
feel uncertain about progression, communication and
creating inclusive learning environments. Previous
research suggests that disability-specific coach
education remains limited, with many coaches relying

Abstract
Wheelchair tennis continues to grow internationally, increasing the demand for coaches who can
confidently introduce new players to the sport. While research has considerably advanced our
understanding of wheelchair tennis biomechanics, physiology and elite performance, comparatively
little academic guidance exists to support coaches working with beginners. Introducing new players
presents unique challenges, as players must learn wheelchair mobility, racket skills and tactical
awareness concurrently within an unfamiliar sporting environment. This article provides practical,
evidence-informed recommendations for planning and delivering beginner wheelchair tennis sessions.
Drawing on research from wheelchair tennis, skill acquisition, ecological dynamics, inclusive coaching
and disability sport, alongside applied coaching practice, the paper outlines strategies for preparing
the first session, developing wheelchair mobility, introducing fundamental tennis skills, designing
representative practice, adapting coaching to individual needs and promoting confidence and longterm participation. The recommendations are intended to help coaches create enjoyable, inclusive
and player-centred learning environments that support both immediate engagement and sustained
involvement in wheelchair tennis.

Wheelchair tennis is also much more than learning a

psychological wellbeing and social connectedness

should therefore aim to develop enjoyment, confidence
and belonging alongside technical and tactical skills.

Understanding the beginner wheelchair
tennis player
One of the first things coaches learn is that there is
no such thing as a "typical" beginner. Players arrive
with different impairments, levels of wheelchair
experience, sporting backgrounds and personal
goals. Some may have used a wheelchair for many
years or competed in other wheelchair sports, while
others may be using a sports wheelchair for the first
time. Likewise, some participants may have previous
tennis experience before acquiring an impairment,
whereas others are completely new to racket sports.
These differences mean that effective coaching starts
by understanding the individual rather than making
assumptions based on diagnosis or classification.

Preparing for the first session
The quality of a beginner's first experience often
determines whether they return. Long before the first
ball is struck, coaches influence this experience through
the way the session is organised, the environment that
is created and the interactions they have with players.
The objective of an introductory session is therefore
not technical perfection but helping every participant
leave the court feeling successful, confident and
motivated to play again. Preparation begins with the
environment. Players should be able to access the
court easily, move safely around the venue and find
equipment ready for use. Sports wheelchairs should
be checked before the session, including tyre pressure,
brakes and general safety, while rackets, lowercompression balls, targets and other equipment should
be organised to minimise unnecessary interruptions.
Good organisation allows more time for meaningful
practice and keeps beginners engaged throughout
the session. The coaching environment is equally
important. A warm welcome, clear explanations
and simple session objectives help reduce anxiety
and establish a positive learning environment from
the outset. Coaches should encourage questions,
reassure players that mistakes are an expected
part of learning and celebrate small improvements
throughout the session. These behaviours help create
the psychological safety that underpins confidence,
exploration and long-term participation (Deci &
Ryan, 2000; Ryan & Deci, 2020). Communication
should remain collaborative throughout practice.

coaches should ask questions, listen carefully and

confidence and physical capability. Small adjustments
to equipment, court size or task difficulty often have
a greater impact on learning than extensive technical
instruction. This flexible, player-centred approach

Teaching wheelchair mobility before
tennis skills
Confident wheelchair movement underpins every
aspect of wheelchair tennis. Before players can recover
effectively, prepare for a shot or make good tactical
decisions, they must first learn how to move efficiently
around the court. For beginner coaches, this means
treating wheelchair mobility as a performance skill
in its own right rather than simply a way of getting
from one shot to the next. Asking beginners to master
the movement skills all at once can quickly reduce
confidence. A more effective approach is to establish
movement confidence first before progressively
integrating tennis-specific demands. Early sessions
should therefore focus on simple movement challenges
that encourage players to become comfortable in
the sports wheelchair. Activities such as obstacle
courses, relay races, reaction games and follow-theleader exercises provide enjoyable opportunities to
practise accelerating, braking, turning and recovering
while avoiding the monotony of isolated movement
drills. These activities also give coaches valuable
opportunities to observe each player's confidence,
balance and movement strategies before introducing
more complex tasks.
As movement confidence develops, coaches should
gradually combine wheelchair mobility with racket
and ball activities. Catching, throwing and striking
tasks encourage players to coordinate movement with
and decision-making simultaneously. The aim is
not to produce technically perfect strokes but to
help players develop confidence, consistency and an
understanding of how to play the game. Forehand
and backhand strokes usually provide the starting
point because they form the basis of rallying. Rather
than concentrating on detailed technical instruction,
coaches should initially encourage players to establish
a comfortable grip, make consistent contact in front of
the body and recover into a balanced court position
after every shot. Simple external coaching cues, such
as "hit over the net", "aim for the target" or "recover
ready for the next ball", are often more effective than
providing lengthy technical explanations. Directing
attention towards the intended outcome of the
movement has consistently been shown to enhance
motor learning and performance (Wulf, 2013).
Practice should also encourage players to move while
striking the ball. Learning to hit stationary balls
without considering court position may produce
short-term technical improvements but does little
to prepare beginners for the demands of match play.
Instead, coaches should gradually integrate movement,
perception and decision-making from the earliest
stages of learning. Representative practice allows
players to develop technical, tactical and perceptual
skills together while learning to adapt to changing
situations on court (Davids et al., 2008; Pinder et
al., 2011; Button et al., 2020). Serving should also
ball tracking and decision-making, helping mobility
and tennis skills develop together rather than
separately. Research shows that learning is enhanced
when practice resembles the game players are
preparing for (Davids et al., 2008; Pinder et al., 2011).
Progression should always be guided by the individual
rather than a fixed coaching plan. Beginners vary
considerably in wheelchair experience, upper-body
strength, trunk control and sporting background, so
the same activity may be appropriate for one player
but overly demanding for another. Small adjustments
to movement distance, task complexity or equipment
are often enough to maintain an appropriate balance
between challenge and success while keeping practice
enjoyable and representative. Figure 2 provides
examples of fundamental wheelchair movement
patterns that can be introduced during the early
stages of learning. Although presented as individual

player's confidence and ability while progressively

For coaches, the challenge is to create activities that
are simple enough for beginners to experience success
while still reflecting the essential demands of the
game. Rather than separating technical practice from
game play, coaches should introduce tennis through
modified activities that encourage players to move,
perceive information and make decisions from the
outset. Simple adaptations, such as reducing court
size, using lower-compression balls or introducing
achievable tactical goals, allow beginners to experience
success without removing the characteristics that
make the activity recognisably tennis.
An effective beginner session also keeps players
moving. Long queues, repetitive basket feeding
and prolonged technical demonstrations reduce
opportunities to practise, make decisions and
develop confidence. Wherever possible, activities
should involve every player simultaneously through
partner work, small groups or station-based practice.
Increasing meaningful repetitions while maintaining
enjoyment is often more valuable than providing
additional technical instruction. Modified games
are particularly effective because they combine
movement, racket skills and tactical awareness
within a single activity. Rather than viewing games
as a reward at the end of practice, coaches should use
them as the primary learning environment from the
beginning. Simple target games encourage accuracy
and recovery, cooperative rallies develop consistency
and court positioning, while small-sided games allow
beginners to experience the rhythm of tennis without
becoming overwhelmed.

Adapting coaching for every player
No two beginner wheelchair tennis players arrive the
same. Some players will move confidently in a sports
wheelchair from their first session, while others may
initially find basic propulsion challenging. Effective
coaching therefore relies on adapting practice to
the individual rather than expecting every player to
progress in the same way. The aim of adaptation is
not to make practice easier but to make it appropriate.
Coaches should create activities that provide every
player with an achievable level of challenge while
preserving the essential characteristics of wheelchair
tennis. A constraints-led approach provides a
practical framework for doing this, allowing coaches
to manipulate equipment, court dimensions, scoring
systems and task objectives to match individual needs
without fundamentally changing the activity (Newell,
1986; Davids et al., 2008). Classification reflects the
impact of impairment on performance, not a player's
confidence, previous experience or capacity to
learn (Tweedy & Vanlandewijck, 2011), and players
within the same classification often demonstrate
very different movement abilities, technical skills
and learning needs. Coaches should therefore
observe how individuals perform and adapt practice
accordingly rather than assuming players with similar
classifications require identical coaching.
Mixed-ability groups are common in community
wheelchair tennis and should be viewed as a coaching
opportunity rather than a problem. Players can remain
within the same activity while working towards
different objectives. One player may be aiming
to maintain a rally, another to recover effectively
after every shot, while a more experienced beginner
focuses on ball placement. These small adjustments
allow everyone to remain engaged without separating
the group or disrupting the flow of practice. Perhaps
the most important adaptation a coach can make
is knowing when to step in and when to step back.
Not every mistake requires immediate correction.
Allowing players time to explore, experiment and solve
movement problems often produces more durable
learning than continually prescribing technical
solutions (Davids et al., 2008; Pinder et al., 2011).

Building
confidence
and
long-term
participation
For most beginners, the first few coaching sessions
are not about winning matches. They are about
deciding whether wheelchair tennis is a sport they
want to continue playing. Coaches therefore have
an important responsibility that extends beyond
teaching technique. The environment they create,
the experiences they provide and the relationships
they build can all influence whether players leave the
court feeling confident enough to return. Research
consistently highlights the importance of competence,
autonomy and relatedness in supporting motivation
and sustained participation (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Ryan &
Deci, 2020). In practice, this means designing sessions
where players experience success, have opportunities
to make decisions and feel part of a welcoming group.
Even small successes (returning a serve or mastering
a turn) can have a significant impact on confidence
during the early stages of learning. The way coaches
provide feedback also shapes the learning experience.
Rather than correcting every mistake, coaches should
encourage players to explore solutions and reflect on
what worked well. Simple questions such as "What
helped you reach that ball?", "What did you notice?" or
"Where could you recover next?" encourage players to
think and solve problems.
Equally important is the social environment. Many
people begin wheelchair tennis to improve fitness,
regain confidence or meet others with similar
experiences. Participation has been associated with
improvements in confidence, wellbeing and social
connectedness (Richardson et al., 2017). Coaches can
strengthen these outcomes by encouraging interaction
between players, celebrating individual progress and
creating a club environment where everyone feels
welcome regardless of experience or ability. For
beginner coaches, the message is simple: players are
far more likely to continue if they enjoy the experience
than if they simply execute technically correct strokes.
Successful coaching should be judged by more than
technical improvement. The most successful beginner
sessions are those in which players leave the court
feeling more confident than when they arrived and
looking forward to coming back next week.
Common coaching mistakes
Everyone can make mistakes when introducing
beginners to wheelchair tennis. Most are not caused by
poor coaching, but by transferring coaching methods
that work in able-bodied tennis without considering
the additional demands of wheelchair mobility.
Recognising these common pitfalls helps coaches
create more effective, enjoyable and inclusive learning
experiences. One of the most common mistakes is
focusing on stroke technique before players can
move confidently around the court. Without effective
wheelchair control, players struggle to prepare for the
ball, recover after shots and make consistent contact.
Developing confidence in propulsion, turning and
recovery should therefore take priority during the
early stages of learning (Goosey-Tolfrey & Moss, 2005;
Sindall et al., 2013).
Another frequent mistake is trying to correct
everything. Beginners are already learning a lot of skills.
Too much technical instruction can quickly become
overwhelming. Instead, coaches should identify one
or two priority coaching points and allow players time
to explore different movement solutions. Session

Conclusion
Introducing beginners to wheelchair tennis involves
far more than teaching strokes. Coaches are helping
players learn to move confidently in a sports
wheelchair, develop racket skills, solve tactical
problems and, perhaps most importantly, enjoy the
experience of playing the game. The first few coaching
sessions often shape whether a player chooses to
continue participating, making the quality of beginner
coaching fundamental to the long-term growth
of wheelchair tennis. This article has presented
practical, evidence-informed recommendations for
planning and delivering beginner sessions by bringing
together research from wheelchair tennis, disability
sport, skill acquisition and inclusive coaching with
applied coaching practice. Although every player
follows a different developmental journey, several
principles remain consistent: prioritise movement
before technique, design representative and enjoyable
practice activities, adapt coaching to the individual
and create environments where players feel confident
to explore, make mistakes and learn. As wheelchair
tennis continues to grow internationally, coaches
will play an increasingly important role in ensuring
that new players experience the sport in a positive,
inclusive and enjoyable way. Technical development
will always matter, but lasting participation begins
with confidence, enjoyment and a sense of belonging.
