Abstract
Success in wheelchair tennis depends upon far more than technical stroke production. Players must
continually perceive opportunities, create and exploit space, manage time and make effective tactical
decisions while simultaneously controlling wheelchair movement. Although tactical understanding is
fundamental to successful performance, relatively little guidance exists to help coaches teach tactical
principles within wheelchair tennis. Existing literature has predominantly focused on biomechanics,
physiology and performance analysis, leaving a gap between research and applied coaching practice.
This article provides practical, evidence-informed recommendations for coaching tactical awareness
in wheelchair tennis. Drawing upon research from wheelchair tennis, skill acquisition, ecological
dynamics and representative learning design, alongside applied coaching experience, the paper
outlines fundamental tactical principles including understanding space, using the two-bounce rule,
attacking and defensive play, serve and return tactics, and representative approaches for developing
tactical decision-making. Practical coaching recommendations, court diagrams and practical coaching
activities are presented throughout to support coaches working across participation and performance
environments.
Key words: wheelchair tennis, tactics, coaching, representative practice.

The two-bounce rule: creating time as a
tactical advantage
The two-bounce rule is one of the defining tactical
features of wheelchair tennis. While newcomers often
view the second bounce simply as a rule adaptation,
experienced players recognise it as one of the sport's
greatest tactical advantages. Rather than providing
an additional opportunity to reach the ball, the
second bounce creates time, improves court position
and enables players to make more effective tactical
decisions. Coaches should therefore teach it as a
tactical tool rather than a safety net.
Unlike able-bodied tennis, where players generally
attempt to take every ball after the first bounce,
wheelchair tennis requires players to decide whether
striking the ball early or intentionally allowing the
second bounce offers the greatest advantage. This
decision depends on factors such as court position,
wheelchair momentum, the quality of the incoming
shot and the player's ability to recover. Tactical
success lies not in always using one bounce or two, but
in recognising which option best suits the situation.
Taking the ball after the first bounce is often
appropriate when players are well positioned or
receive a shorter, slower ball. By intercepting the
ball early, players reduce the opponent's available
time, create wider angles and maintain attacking
pressure. Conversely, allowing the second bounce can
be the more effective option when recovering from
previous movement, defending wide positions or
responding to deep, penetrating shots. The additional
time allows players to reposition the wheelchair,
regain balance and prepare a higher-quality return.
Given the importance of movement efficiency in
wheelchair tennis, effective recovery and positioning
are fundamental to successful tactical performance
(Goosey-Tolfrey & Moss, 2005; Sindall et al., 2013).

Understanding tactics in wheelchair
tennis
One of the biggest differences between novice and
experienced players is not how well they strike the ball,
but how well they understand the game. Successful
wheelchair tennis players continually interpret
information from the environment, anticipate their
opponent's intentions and adapt their movement
and shot selection accordingly. Every rally therefore
becomes a process of solving tactical problems rather
than simply producing technically correct strokes.
At its simplest, tactical decision-making revolves
around three questions:
•	 Where is the available space?
•	 How much time do I have?
•	 What movement solution gives me the greatest
tactical advantage?
Players answer these questions repeatedly throughout
every rally by interpreting information from the
opponent, the ball and their own court position. From
an ecological dynamics perspective, tactical decisions
emerge through the interaction between the player
and the performance environment rather than from
pre-planned movement patterns (Araújo et al., 2006;
Davids et al., 2013).
Wheelchair tennis adds another layer of tactical
complexity because every decision must also consider
wheelchair movement. Players must coordinate
propulsion, turning, recovery and stroke preparation
while maintaining effective court positioning, making
movement efficiency a fundamental component of
tactical performance (Goosey-Tolfrey & Moss, 2005;
Sindall et al., 2013).
The two-bounce rule further distinguishes wheelchair
tennis from the able-bodied game. Rather than simply
adapting the rules, it creates additional tactical
opportunities by allowing players to create time,
recover more effectively and improve court position.

Attacking principles
Successful attacking play in wheelchair tennis is
rarely the result of a single powerful shot. Instead,
players build pressure by controlling time and space
until an attacking opportunity emerges. Coaches
should therefore encourage players to think beyond
hitting winners and instead understand how rallies
are constructed.
The first objective of attacking play is to reduce the
opponent's available time. Players can achieve this
by taking the ball earlier, moving forwards when
appropriate or directing shots that force immediate
movement. Importantly, reducing time does not
necessarily require greater ball speed. Well-directed
shots with depth or angle often create more pressure
than simply hitting harder.
Creating space is equally important. Effective
attackers move opponents away from the centre of
the court, force repeated changes of direction and
exploit the space that follows. Deep groundstrokes are
particularly valuable because they restrict attacking
options, provide additional recovery time and often
produce shorter replies that can be attacked. Research
in tennis consistently highlights the importance
of controlling court position through depth before
attempting aggressive finishing shots (Crespo & Reid,
2007; Reid et al., 2007).

Defensive principles
Effective defence is more than simply keeping the
ball in play. Skilled defenders use defensive situations
to create time, recover court position and gradually
regain tactical control. Coaches should therefore
present defence as an active tactical strategy rather
than simply a response to pressure.
The primary objective of defensive play is to create
sufficient time to recover. Height and depth often
become more valuable than pace, as higher, deeper
shots increase ball flight time, allowing players to
reposition the wheelchair while reducing immediate
attacking opportunities for the opponent.
The two-bounce rule is particularly valuable in
defensive situations. Rather than rushing to intercept
every ball after one bounce, players should recognise
when intentionally allowing the second bounce
provides more time to recover, establish balance and
produce a higher-quality return. Used effectively, the
second bounce can transform defensive situations
into neutral rallies instead of rushed, low-percentage
shots.

requires anticipation. As players recover, they should
continually interpret the opponent's court position,

to apply them within realistic game situations.

playing styles. Encouraging adaptable decisionmaking, rather than one ideal solution, is particularly

mobility and wheelchair handling inevitably influence
tactical choices.

Serve and return
Every point in wheelchair tennis begins with an
opportunity to establish tactical control. Rather than
treating the serve and return as isolated technical
skills, coaches should present them as the opening
phase of a tactical sequence that influences court
position, movement and the direction of the rally.
Following the serve, players must transition quickly
from stroke production to wheelchair propulsion and
recovery. Efficient movement after the serve is just as
important as the quality of the serve itself, enabling
players to establish an effective court position for the
next shot.
Introducing the concept of Serve +1 encourages
players to think beyond simply starting the rally. The
serve should create an opportunity for the following
shot. A wide serve may open space on the opposite side
of the court, a body serve can restrict the opponent's
swing, and a deep serve may limit attacking return
options. Coaches should therefore encourage players
to decide where they intend to play their next shot
before beginning the service action.
The return of serve should be approached with the
same tactical intent. Rather than simply returning
the ball into play, players should aim to neutralise
the server's advantage or create immediate pressure.
Consistent, deep returns directed towards larger
target areas often provide the best opportunity to
regain tactical balance while allowing sufficient time
to recover. As players develop, greater variation in
direction, height and pace can be introduced.
Observation is equally important. Recognising an
opponent's serving patterns, preferred placements
and recovery habits enables players to anticipate likely
outcomes and make more effective tactical decisions
before the ball is struck.
These skills are best developed through representative
practice. Coaches should design activities that include
complete Serve +1 and Return +1 sequences, rewarding
effective recovery, first-shot quality and tactical
decision-making rather than simply successful serves.
By linking technical execution with tactical intention
from the beginning of every rally, players develop a
more complete understanding of how to establish and
maintain tactical control.

A second pitfall is over-coaching. Continuous
instruction can limit exploration and encourage
players to rely on the coach rather than learning
to interpret information independently. Instead,
questioning and guided discovery should direct
attention towards relevant tactical cues while allowing
players to develop their own solutions (Renshaw et al.,

Coaches should also be careful not to reward
outcomes over decisions. Winning a point does not
always reflect good tactical decision-making, just as
a well-considered decision may not always produce
immediate success. Reinforcing intelligent choices,
regardless of the outcome, encourages better longterm tactical development.
Practice design is equally important. Long queues,
repetitive basket feeding and isolated stroke drills
reduce opportunities for perception, movement and
decision-making. In contrast, representative practice
activities that require players to interpret opponents,
move efficiently and solve tactical problems are more
likely to transfer to match play (Davids et al., 2008;
Mounter, 2025; Mounter et al., 2025).
Finally, coaches should avoid expecting every
player to solve tactical problems in the same way.

Common coaching pitfalls and better
practice
Developing tactical understanding requires more than
exposing players to match play. Coaches shape how
players perceive information, solve problems and make
decisions throughout every rally. However, several
common coaching practices can unintentionally limit
tactical development. Recognising these pitfalls helps
coaches create learning environments that encourage
adaptable
decision-making
rather
than
simply
improving technical execution.
One of the most common mistakes is treating tactics
as something that should be introduced only after
technique has been mastered. In reality, technical
and tactical skills develop together. Players must
learn not only how to execute a stroke, but also when,
why and where to use it. Developing these elements
simultaneously within representative practice better
reflects the demands of competition (Davids et al.,
2008; Pinder et al., 2011; Button et al., 2020).

Conclusion
Successful wheelchair tennis depends on more than
technical proficiency or physical preparation. Players
ultimately succeed through their ability to perceive
information, create and exploit space, manage time
and adapt their decisions to the changing demands of
each rally. Tactical understanding should therefore be
developed alongside technical and physical skills from
the earliest stages of player development.
This article has presented practical, evidence-informed
recommendations for developing tactical awareness in
wheelchair tennis by integrating current research with
applied coaching practice. Understanding how to use
the two-bounce rule, balance attacking and defensive
play, develop Serve +1 and Return +1 patterns, and
design representative learning environments provides
coaches with a practical framework that can be
adapted across different ages, abilities and stages of
development.

telling players where to hit the ball but creating
learning environments in which they learn to
