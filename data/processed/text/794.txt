Wheelchair tennis was first introduced in 1976,
marking 2026 as the 50th anniversary of the sport.
Wimbledon incorporated wheelchair tennis in 2005
with men’s wheelchair doubles, today the competition
includes men’s, women’s and quads for both singles
and doubles. Wimbledon is the only Grand Slam
tournament played on a natural surface, grass.
Although courts are carefully prepared by ground staff,
surface hardness and rolling characteristics can vary
throughout a tournament due to wear and weather
conditions. Wheelchair tennis can be described as the
interaction between the tennis player, wheelchair,
racket, and playing surface (grass, clay, and hard
court) (Rietveld, Vegter, et al., 2021). In general,
grass can introduce up to five times higher rolling
resistance compared to hardcourt, directly influencing
court performance (Rietveld, Mason, et al., 2021). To
overcome these high rolling resistances on grass,
players need to increase efforts which elevates the risk
of shoulder injuries. Wheelchair configuration can be
adapted to the playing surface, with castors and tyres
having a direct impact (Rietveld, Vegter, et al., 2021).
The wheelchair tennis season is rapidly increasing
in length, therefore, understanding the demands of
different surfaces and levels is essential for health and
performance of wheelchair tennis players (Williamson
et al., 2024). Qualitative investigation into player

Discussions among participants highlighted the need
to manage and prioritise player welfare (i.e., physical
injury and fatigue), changes to the wheelchair setup, and perceived demands (Figure 1). Wheelchair
manufacturers and researchers were also identified
as key contributors to the players’ integrated support
team (Rietveld et al., 2026). There is a misconception
about wheelchair tennis players damaging grass
courts. Players and support staff identified worn
castor wheels (sharp edges) and tipping over as the
main concerns for potential grass court damage.
To address these concerns, structured education and
standardised rules could lead to a positive shift to
alleviate concerns around potential court damage.
One approach is to implement chair regulations at
grass court tournaments, such as standardising castor
design and covering sharp edges to minimise court
damage (Figure 1) (Loughborough University, 2024).
Wimbledon has recently implemented such court
protection regulations for castor wheels into the Grand
Slam Rule book (Grand Slam Tennis, 2026). Future,
work should continue to explore inclusivity and
accessibility within wheelchair tennis. Perspectives
should be captured across all levels of the sport, from
grassroots participation to elite performance.
