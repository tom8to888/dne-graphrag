Introduction
Wheelchair tennis was first introduced in 1972, and
the popularity and interest in the sport has grown ever
since (Diaper and Goosey-Tolfrey 2009). The rules
in wheelchair tennis are similar to the able-bodied
equivalent, except for the allowance of two bounces
and the use of a wheelchair. Wheelchair tennis is
well integrated on the international tennis tour, with
all Grand slams running a wheelchair tennis event.
This has introduced different surface interactions
with Roland Garros played on clay and Wimbledon
on grass. Despite two of the main tournaments
on the international tour being played on clay
and grass, limited knowledge exists on wheelchair

Abstract
Purpose: To investigate the effect of different tyre types on wheelchair tennis mobility on clay courts.
Methods: Four elite wheelchair tennis players performed a coast down and Butterfly-sprint tests using
various three tyre types (Tufo, IRC, Schwalbe) on a French clay court. All sports wheelchairs were
equipped with three inertial measurement units (mounted on both wheels and frame) to measure
linear and rotational velocities as well as accelerations. A Friedman test was conducted with wheel
type (Tufo, IRC, Schwalbe) as the within-subject measure. Results: Significant differences in rolling
resistances were observed across tyre types (p=0.05, W=0.75). Rolling resistance was highest for Tufo
(13.38±1.92N), followed by IRC (12.85±1.66N) and lowest for Schwalbe tyres (11.49±1.67N). Performance
in Butterfly-sprint test also varied between tyre types (p=0.04, W=0.81), with IRC producing fastest
times (15.14±1.20s) compared to Schwalbe (15.45±1.08s) and Tufo (15.49±1.31s). Conclusions: Schwalbe
tyres may reduce linear rolling resistance, while IRC tyres may enhance mobility performance in
wheelchair tennis players. These findings suggest that wheelchair set-up, particularly tyre selection
should be adapted according to the player surface.

Wheelchair tennis can be described as the interaction
between the wheelchair athlete, the wheelchair
configuration and the environment (Rietveld et al.
2021b). Components directly influencing wheelchairenvironment interactions are the main wheels, tyres
and castor wheels (Rietveld et al. 2021b). Qualitative
research previously described the need for players to
adapt tyre pressure to the surface competed on (Mason
et al. 2010). A mixed-method investigation on grass
showed perceptions of players and support staff on
improved performance using tyres with bigger tread
sizes and lower tyre pressures (Rietveld et al. 2026).
Protocol
A standardised coast-down test and wheelchair tennis
drill were completed during a one-day experiment
on a French clay court. During the coast-down test,
the player completed two strong pushes and leaned
slightly forward, allowing the wheelchair to decelerate
naturally to calculate rolling resistance. Wheelchair
mobility performance (ability on court) was assessed
by a wheelchair tennis specific field test, a ButterflySprint test (Rietveld et al. 2019) (Figure 1). Inter-trial
reliability for this specific field test was previously
defined as excellent (ICC=0.95) (Rietveld et al. 2019).
The Butterfly-sprint test specifically assesses chair
rotational velocity at higher speeds, and linear
velocities in a straight line. Verbal encouragements by
the lead investigator were given during all tests. Three
trials per tyre type were completed using inertial
measurement
units
(IMUs.
Movesense, Vantaa,
Finland) on the middle-bar frame and inside the hub
of both wheels. The IMU data enabled analysis of end
time, and linear/rotational velocity and acceleration.
Pilot data of this study echoed these results showing
improved rotational performance with IRC tyres with
72mm castor wheels (Rietveld et al. 2026)
Key
wheelchair
mobility
performance
metrics
in wheelchair tennis are rotations on the spot,
rotations while in motion, and linear velocities and
accelerations (Rietveld et al. 2023). Standardised field
tests to evaluate these wheeling performance metrics
have been developed (Rietveld et al. 2019) Several
case studies have shown that sprint times are reduced
in the first 10m on clay compared to hardcourt
(Rietveld et al. 2021a; Sánchez-Pay and Sanz-Rivas
2021). An agility sprint test echoed results, with
reduced rotational performances on clay compared
to hardcourt (Sánchez-Pay and Sanz-Rivas 2021).
Clay courts led to a 1.5 times higher rolling resistance
compared to hardcourt (Rietveld et al. 2021a). To
compensate for these increased resistances and loss
of performance, it was recommended to change tyres
based on surfaces competed on. However, despite
these recommendations, no study has systematically
examined the effect of tyre type on wheelchair tennis
performance on clay courts.  Therefore, the purpose
of this study was to investigate the effect of different
tyre types on wheelchair tennis mobility on clay
courts, addressing the lack of empirical evidence in
optimal tyre selection. It was hypothesised that tyres
with great tread of thickness would improve mobility
performance by reducing the negative effects of
increased rolling resistance, in line with findings from
grass courts.
Methods
Participants
Four elite wheelchair tennis players took part in
this study (3 Open Male, 1 Quad). Average age of the
participants was 24±6 yrs with an experience of 9±6
yrs. Ethical approval was provided by the local ethics
committee of Loughborough University (ID_14290).
Participants provided written informed consent prior
to taking part.
Design
All participants used their own tennis sports
wheelchairs, testing a variety of tyre types (Tufo,
IRC, Schwalbe). Tufo is a high-pressure tubeless tyre
(23mm), current set-up of all players. IRC and Schwalbe
are clincher tyres with bigger treads of 25mm. The
Schwalbe tyre had bigger tread grooves compared to
the smoother treads of IRC or Tufo tyres. Both clincher
tyres were tested based on recommendations from
a wheelchair configuration specialist that it could
provide better grip on clay. All tyres were inflated to
recommended pressures (200 PSI for Tufo and 130 PSI
for IRC/Schwalbe). Wheel diameters were 0.69m with
a camber angle of 20 degrees.

Data analysis
Data were collected at 100Hz, low-pass filtered (2nd
order, 10Hz) and analysed using custom-written
Python scripts (de Klerk et al. 2023). Synchronisation
between sensors were determined based on timestamps
at the start of each trial. The deceleration values of
the coast-down tests were calculated using the slope
of the velocity curve. Rolling resistance was calculated
wheelchair mobility metrics of the Butterfly sprint
test. Kendall W effect sizes were determined, using
Cohen’s interpretation guidelines, indicating small
(0.1-0.3), moderate (0.3-0.5) and large effect sizes
(>=0.5) (Cohen 1977). Significance was set at p<0.05
for all tests.
Results
The coast down tests showed differences in rolling
resistances across tyre types (p=0.05, W=0.75) with
resistances being 13.38±1.92N for Tufo, 12.85±1.66N
for IRC and 11.49±1.67N for Schwalbe tyres. Quality
of the coast down trials showed a mean root mean
squared error of 0.03-0.04 and R2 of 0.97-0.98.
There was a significant difference in end times on
the butterfly sprint test between tyre types with IRC
showing quicker end times (15.14±1.20s) compared to
Tufo (15.49±1.31s) and Schwalbe (15.45±1.08) (p=0.04,
W=0.81). Wheelchair mobility metrics showed a
significant difference in percentage of time spend
above 90 deg·s-1 across tyre types (p=0.04, W=0.81,

Discussion
Changing tyres in wheelchair tennis can positively
impact performance in wheelchair tennis by reducing
rolling resistance and improving wheelchair mobility
on clay courts. While the Schwalbe tyre demonstrated
lowest rolling resistance, the IRC tyre resulted in the
fastest completed times on the Butterfly-Sprint test,
alongside greater time spent at rotational velocities
above 90 deg·s-1. The magnitude of these differences
was supported by high effect sizes, highlighting
the
complexity
of
wheelchair-user-environment
interactions. These findings suggest that optimal
performance on clay courts depends on a balancing
rolling resistance with effective wheelchair mobility
performance.
The lower rolling resistance for the Schwalbe tyres, with
feature larger tread grooves, shows a reduced rolling
resistance for straight line propulsion. Conversely, the
smoother IRC tyre may facilitate improved rotational
movement. Enhanced rotational performance on an
agility-sprint tests, such as the Butterfly sprint test,
has a direct impact on wheelchair tennis mobility.
Rotational performance is a key part of wheelchair
tennis mobility game play (van der Slikke et al. 2020;
Rietveld et al. 2023). These results are consistent with
perceptions of wheelchair tennis players on grass,
with wheelchair tennis players perceiving IRC tyres to
lead to improved wheeling performance (Rietveld et
al. 2026).
There is a continued focus on chair set-ups, with
recent castor wheel regulations added to the Grand
Slam Rulebook (Grand Slam Tennis 2026). Bigger
castor wheels also showed to reduce rolling resistance
across various surfaces (Deves et al. 2025; Rietveld et
al. 2026).  Further changing wheelchair configuration
depends on the physical demands of wheelchair tennis
across surface types, which is a knowledge gap still
need to be filled (Williamson et al. 2024)  This also
sheds the light on more individualised approaches,
being beneficial for certain style players or across
classification levels.
Practical Applications and limitations
It is recommended that wheelchair tennis players
consider using slightly thicker tyres and prioritise
chair set-up adjustments to reduce strain and enhance
performance. However, the small sample size limits
statistical power, restricting further generalisation
of these findings. Nevertheless, small differences on
a short agility drill can be seen as a positive result,
as adapting to a new set-up usually takes time (de
Groot et al. 2018). Appropriate tyre selection may help
reduce some of the challenges of playing wheelchair
tennis on various surfaces (clay, grass, hardcourt).
Future research should include larger cohorts and
further explore the interaction between wheelchair
configuration, tyre and castor wheel design during
wheelchair tennis match play performance.
Conclusions
Schwalbe tyres may reduce linear rolling resistance,
while IRC tyres appear to enhance wheelchair
mobility performance in wheelchair tennis players.
These findings support the recommendation to
adjust wheelchair configurations based on playing
surface. Future research incorporating larger sample
sizes, longitudinal designs and more individualised
customisation may provide further insight into
the interaction between player, wheelchair and the
environment on different surfaces.
