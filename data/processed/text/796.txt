Introduction
Wheelchair tennis began in the mid-1970s, when
Brad Parks started experimenting with tennis from a
wheelchair after a skiing accident left him paraplegic
(Paralympic, 2012). By the 1980s, wheelchair tennis
had spread beyond the United States and was
being promoted through clinics, exhibitions, and
tournaments in other regions. The International
Wheelchair Tennis Federation was formed in the
late 1980s, and the sport was later integrated into
the International Tennis Federation in 1998 (Lockie,
2026). Wheelchair tennis became a full medal sport at
the Paralympic Games in Barcelona in 1992. Over time,
it was also added to all four Grand Slam tournaments,
with Roland Garros becoming the last to integrate
wheelchair events in 2007 (Paralympic, 2012). Today,
it is a global sport with more than 160 tournaments
across more than 40 countries.

Abstract
This literature review synthesizes recent scientific evidence (2016–2026) regarding elite performance
and training in wheelchair tennis. By systematically analyzing peer-reviewed experimental studies, it
examines the sport's competitive evolution, characterized by increased point duration, shot frequency,
and intermittent high-intensity demands. Results highlight the significant influence of court surfaces
on tactical requirements and the critical role of trunk function as the primary determinant of mobility,
acceleration, and hitting power. Beyond physical conditioning, this review integrates emerging
evidence on neurocognitive efficiency, emphasizing core executive functions like inhibitory control,
and the necessity of a socio-environmental paradigm shift. Furthermore, the effectiveness of targeted
interventions, such as High-Intensity Interval Training (HIIT) and specialized strength circuit models, is
demonstrated for optimizing athletes' metabolic and musculoskeletal capacities. The study concludes
with practical guidelines for coaches, focusing on task design specificity and wheelchair configuration
optimization to improve overall player performance. By combining task-specific conditioning, wheelchair
configuration optimization, and psychosocial interventions, practitioners can foster resilience and
improve overall performance in this complex, evolving sport.
Key words: wheelchair tennis, match performance, training interventions, literature review.

Design and Search Strategy
A structured literature review was conducted in
June 2026 to synthesize recent evidence regarding
performance and training in wheelchair tennis. The
search was carried out using Google Scholar, restricting
results to peer-reviewed articles and experimental
studies published between January 2016 and June
2026, in terms of adding new information to previous
reviews in wheelchair tennis (Villaplana-Velasco &
Fuentes-García, 2015). To ensure a comprehensive
identification of relevant literature, the following
search string was utilized: [allintitle: "wheelchair
tennis" (performance OR training OR competition
OR match OR elite OR paralympic) -"table tennis"
-"review"].
Google Scholar was selected as the search database
due to its broad interdisciplinary coverage and
high search sensitivity, which are advantageous for
capturing relevant literature in wheelchair tennis
research. Furthermore, its open-access nature and
user-friendly interface facilitate the reproducibility of
the search strategy and allow practitioners, including
coaches, to replicate the search by copying the search
string.
Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
To align with the practical purpose of this synthesis,
studies were selected based on the following criteria:
•	 Inclusion: Peer-reviewed original research and
experimental designs focused on wheelchair
tennis (singles or doubles) at recreational or
professional levels; studies examining match/
competition demands, physiological profiles,
biomechanics, or specific training interventions;
and publications written in English or Spanish.
•	 Exclusion:
Documented
literature
reviews,
validation
studies,
social
or
psychological
programs
unrelated
to
physical/tactical
performance, and studies published prior to 2016.

Furthermore, regarding physical performance, recent

Beyond the physical environment, contemporary
sports science emphasizes the need to evaluate
wheelchair tennis through a holistic lens that
integrates socio-environmental and neurocognitive
paradigms (Leale et al., 2025). From a socioenvironmental
perspective,
athletes
often
face
systemic barriers and ableist ideologies that restrict
athletic development, leading current researchers to
advocate for the human rights model of disability,
a framework derived from the United Nations
Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities
(CRPD) that promotes equity and integration based
on ability level rather than disability status (Altomare
et al., 2026). Concurrently, as an open-skill sport,
wheelchair tennis demands extreme neurocognitive
efficiency where elite performance relies heavily
on core executive functions—specifically inhibitory
control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility to
manage real-time tactical adjustments and suppress
emotional impulses under intense spatio-temporal
constraints (Di Muccio & Grossenbacher, 2026).
This neurocognitive load is deeply intertwined
with the intermittent, high-intensity nature of the
sport, where players cover almost 4 kilometres per
match at an average intensity of 75% of their peak
heart rate (Williamson et al., 2024). To optimize
training conditioning for these specific metabolic
and mechanical demands, integrated programming
utilizing High-Intensity Interval Training (HIIT) and
specialized strength circuit models has emerged as
a crucial methodology. Targeted HIIT interventions,
which encompass multi-directional accelerations
and reactive agility drills performed with the racket
in hand, have proven highly effective in concurrently
enhancing
cardiorespiratory
fitness,
upper-body
musculoskeletal strength, and sport-specific agility
(Yulianto et al., 2024).
Since the bibliographic review from Villaplana-Velasco
& Fuentes-García (2015), there is a need to synthesize
the most recent scientific evidence on performance
and training in wheelchair tennis to optimize practical
interventions. Consequently, the aim of this study
is to compile and analyse recent scientific literature
to identify the key factors influencing athlete
performance, with particular emphasis on their direct
implications for the design of training programs.
Influence of Court Surface
The playing surface modulates the external load
and tactical approach. Hard courts involve the
highest external load, characterized by longer match
durations and a greater number of strokes (SánchezPay
&
Sanz-Rivas,
2021b).
However,
research
indicates that matches lasting less than 90 minutes
do not induce significant neuromuscular fatigue or
performance decrements (Ponzano & Gollin, 2017a).
Notably, playing on hard courts provides an acute
coordinative stimulus that significantly improves
non-linear sprinting speed (by approximately 2%)
immediately post-match (Ponzano & Gollin, 2017a).
In contrast, while clay courts require greater technical
mastery to manage friction, they do not produce
similar post-match speed gains (Ponzano & Gollin,
2017a). Tactically, grass courts favor aggressive play,
characterized by a higher frequency of aces and netbased shots such as volleys (Sánchez-Pay & SanzRivas, 2021b).
Mobility, Trunk Function, and Biomechanics
Wheelchair mobility performance is defined by six
main components: four rotational (curves and turns
to both racket and non-racket sides) and two linear
(velocity and acceleration) (Rietveld et al., 2023). Trunk
function has been identified as the core of mobility,
serving as a stronger predictor of acceleration and
rotational speed than isolated arm strength (van der
Slikke et al., 2026). Athletes with full trunk control
achieve significantly higher maximal speeds (4.1 m/s)
compared to those with no functional trunk control
(3.2 m/s) (van der Slikke et al., 2026). Furthermore,
the use of a racket during propulsion reduces maximal
speed by 5.3% and alters trunk kinematics (van der
Slikke et al., 2026).
Biomechanically, elite serve performance relies
on
specialized
neuromuscular
coordination.
Electromyographic analysis shows that Paralympic
champions possess more refined muscle synergies,
four compared to three in sub-elite players, specifically
utilizing a synergy in the early takeback phase
(involving the lower trapezius and triceps brachii) to
optimize trunk rotation and energy transfer (Hakariya
et al., 2026).

Results
The initial literature search yielded a total of 47
records. After examining the titles and abstracts and
assessing the full articles according to the eligibility
criteria, a final selection of 17 peer-reviewed studies
was included for this evidence synthesis. The included
articles covered a broad methodological spectrum to
provide a structured and comprehensive overview for
tennis coaches and sports scientists. The extracted
evidence has been categorized into four thematic
areas: (1) competitive evolution and activity profiles,
(2) tactical and physical influence of court surfaces,
(3) mobility, trunk function, and biomechanical
parameters, and (4) effectiveness of specific training
interventions.
 Competitive Evolution and Activity Profiles
Professional wheelchair tennis has undergone a
significant evolution in its physical and technical
demands. Longitudinal analysis of Paralympic Games
from Athens 2004 to Rio 2016 reveals that point
duration has increased from 7.4 s to approximately 9 s,
with a corresponding increase in shots per rally from
3.5 to 4.5 (Sánchez-Pay & Sanz-Rivas, 2021a). Match
play is characterized by intermittent, multidirectional
activity, typically lasting between 50 and 80 minutes
(Sánchez-Pay & Sanz-Rivas, 2021b).
Activity profiles vary significantly across divisions.
Physical performance measures (speed, distance,
and acceleration) are generally highest in the Men’s
division, followed by Women and Quads (Mason et
al., 2020). Elite male players demonstrate a "vigorous
lifestyle" during competition, with total energy
expenditure ranging from 3,118 to 3,368 kcal/day and
physical activity levels between 2.0 and 2.2 (Ellis et al.,
2023). During match play, heart rates average 125 ± 7
bpm, rarely exceeding 140 bpm, indicating a moderate
cardiovascular demand relative to other wheelchair
sports (Ponzano & Gollin, 2017b). While match result
has a minimal effect on physical activity, losing
players tend to perform more high-speed activity to
stay in rallies, and score margins significantly impact
intensity, sets with a small score margin (≤ 3 games)
elicit higher physical demands (Mason et al., 2020).
Successful performance is further distinguished by
technical efficiency, as winners commit fewer unforced
errors and achieve higher effectiveness in serving and
returning (Sánchez-Pay et al., 2017).
•	 Optimizing Mobility: Given that trunk function
serves as the core driver of acceleration and
rotational
speed,
coaches
must
prioritize
individualized
wheelchair
configurations.
Adjustments such as backrest height and strapping
should be optimized to maximize trunk stability
and rotation, which are stronger predictors of
performance than isolated arm strength (van der
Slikke et al., 2026).
•	 Training with the Racket: Research indicates
that the presence of the racket reduces maximal
propulsion speed by 5.3% and significantly alters
trunk kinematics. Consequently, all agility and
sprint-specific conditioning should be conducted
with the racket in hand to ensure athletes adapt
to the specific technical constraints of match play
(van der Slikke et al., 2026).
•	 Integrative HIIT and Strength: Programming
should
incorporate
High-Intensity
Interval
Training (HIIT) sessions of 10–30 minutes to
improve reactive agility. Furthermore, upperbody overload training performed at least twice
weekly has been demonstrated to enhance
explosive response times, particularly beneficial
for defending against powerful serves (Yulianto et
al., 2024; Salierno et al., 2021).
•	 Holistic Development: Training should extend
beyond
physical
conditioning
to
include
psychosocial components. Structured programs,
such as 8-week developmental protocols, have
been shown to enhance sportive character, moral
attitudes, and resilience, which are critical factors
for sustained high-performance and ethical
decision-making in competitive environments
(Şahinler et al., 2026).
Conclusion
This literature review concludes that modern
wheelchair tennis has evolved into a highly complex,
intermittent
sport,
characterized
by
increasing
competitive demands, with point durations extending
to approximately 9 seconds and rally frequencies
reaching 4.5 strokes. Elite performance relies on a
sophisticated synergy between physical conditioning
and neurocognitive efficiency; trunk control serves
as the primary driver of mobility—proving more
predictive of acceleration and rotational speed than
isolated arm strength—while executive functions,
such as inhibitory control, are essential for tactical
decision-making under pressure.
Furthermore, the sport’s environment exerts a
decisive influence, as surface characteristics modulate
physical exertion and tactical requirements. Notably,
practitioners must account for the mechanical
constraints of the sport, such as the 5.3% reduction
in maximal swing speed caused by holding the racket,
and the human rights-based framework that promotes
equitable
athletic
development.
Consequently,
training must go beyond basic physical conditioning.

the striking arm joints during the serve (Mahdi, 2021).
Regarding cardiovascular responses, High-Intensity

models

Beyond physical gains, training programs also
empowered psychosocial state development, with
an 8-week structured training program significantly
enhancing the sportive character and moral attitudes
of players, fostering fair play and resilience (Şahinler
et al., 2026).
Practical Applications for Coaches
Based on current evidence-based findings, the
following guidelines are recommended for optimizing
wheelchair tennis training:
•	 Drill Design and Simulation: Training tasks must
replicate modern competitive demands to ensure
transferability. Drills should be structured around
sequences lasting approximately 9 seconds,
incorporating an average of 4 to 5 shots per rally.
This specific structure mirrors current elite match
patterns, preparing athletes for the intensified
nature of modern professional play (Sánchez-Pay
& Sanz-Rivas, 2021a).
•	 Work-Rest Ratios: To maintain physiological
specificity and ensure appropriate recovery
intervals, coaches should implement a 1:4 workto-rest ratio during technical and tactical training
sessions. This ratio aligns with the intermittent
demands observed in elite matches, ensuring
athletes are conditioned for the specific energy
system requirements of the sport (Sánchez-Pay &
Sanz-Rivas, 2021b).
•	 Surface-Specific Preparation: Training must be
periodized based on the upcoming tournament
surface, as court characteristics modulate both
physical and tactical requirements:
	▪Grass Courts: Focus should be placed on
explosive serve power and net-play execution
(volleys).
	▪Hard Courts: Programming must emphasize
physical conditioning to withstand higher
external loads and longer match durations.
	▪Clay Courts: Emphasis should be placed on
tactical court positioning to maximize the
advantage of the second bounce rule (SánchezPay & Sanz-Rivas, 2021b; Ponzano & Gollin,
2017b).
