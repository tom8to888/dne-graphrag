Introduction
The ability to use advance information sources to
predict action outcomes, is an important perceptualcognitive skill that underpins expertise in tennis
(Navia et al. 2021). Studies investigating the return of
serve have shown that expert players use information
from a range of sources to predict action outcomes
before unambiguous ball flight information becomes
available (Triolet et al., 2013; Jackson & Mogan;
2007). As advance kinematic information is more
deterministic of action outcomes than contextual
information sources (Müller & Abernethy, 2012),
attunement to this has been extensively studied in
ambulatory tennis (AT) serves. For instance, Farrow
et al. (2005) compared the ability of professional and
novice tennis players to predict serve direction when
responding to progressively occluded video clips of
serves and 'live' serves on court. Professional players

Abstract
The present study investigated expertise differences in the prediction of serve direction in wheelchair
tennis (WT). Using a video-based progressive temporal occlusion paradigm, 16 skilled WT players
(ranked top 100 internationally) and 16 novices predicted serve direction from serves occluded at
four instances - one frame after ball release, at the apex of the ball toss, one frame before racketball contact, and 200 ms post-contact. Response accuracy and gaze behaviour were assessed. Skilled
players significantly outperformed novices in overall response accuracy (56.1% vs. 49.4%). Response
accuracy increased across time of occlusion, with ceiling effects for both groups at the post-contact
occlusion. The expertise by time of occlusion interaction was non-significant, with a consistent expertise
advantage across the pre-contact occlusion points. Fixation count did not differ significantly between
groups; however, novices had longer average fixation durations than skilled players (376.24 ms vs.
307.39 ms). Both groups predominantly fixated on the predicted racket-ball contact zone as a visual
anchor for peripheral kinematic information. These findings underscore the importance of attunement
to early kinematic information sources for effective anticipation of serve direction in WT.
Key words: anticipation, perceptual skills, temporal occlusion, racket sports.

Participants
An a priori power analysis conducted in G*Power 3.1
(Faul et al., 2009) indicated that a total sample of 28
would be required to achieve 80% power to detect a
medium-sized interaction effect (f = .25) in a mixed
effects ANOVA at α = .05. We recruited 32 participants
in total with 16 individuals classified in the skilled
group and 16 classified in the novice group.
Skilled participants were professional WT players

in their respective playing category and had played
at least one international tournament. The novice
group had no prior competitive experience playing AT
or WT but were familiar with the game of tennis and
its rules in general. Further details about participant

performed by three professional WT coaches. One

two servers had prior competitive experience in AT at

suggesting that the time constraints in WT may not
necessitate anticipation. However, in the same study,
faster and more accurate serves resulted in more
defensive return outcomes. Moreover, professional
WT players have described the importance of using
contextual and kinematic information sources to

Experimental research on the attunement of expert WT
players to advance information sources has received
limited attention. Reina et al. (2007) investigated
the differences in visual gaze behaviour and motor
responses between five experienced and seven novice
WT players while they observed live serves and videos
of serves. in both conditions, experienced players spent
significantly more time than novice players observing
the server’s upper body during the ball toss phase.
When viewing live serves, experienced players also
spent significantly more time than novices looking at
the server’s head. A subsequent study by Hunfalvay
and Murray (2018) examined the gaze behaviour of
32 international WT players (ranked top 16 in the
world) while viewing videos of WT serves. During the
preparatory and execution phase of the serve, players
who had only played WT had fewer fixations of longer
duration than players who had also played AT. In both
these studies, response accuracy was not measured
so there remains a clear need to determine whether
expert WT players can judge serve direction at better
than chance level from pre-contact information and
how this relates to gaze behaviour.

the least difference at the latest occlusion. Based
on research by Reina et al. (2007) and Hunfalvay &
Murray (2018), it was additionally hypothesised that
skilled WT players will have fewer fixations of longer
duration than novices.

This generated 48 clips for each server (12 clips per
occlusion condition), yielding 144 test trials in total.
Each trial started with a blank screen for two seconds,
followed by the trial number presented for one second.
After this, a freeze frame of the server’s posture just
prior to raising their non-racket arm for the ball toss
was shown for two seconds to allow sufficient time
for the participant to orient their gaze and assess the
server’s posture. The serve action was then presented
up to the relevant time of occlusion without any
audio. Upon occlusion, a blank screen was shown for
two seconds. In total, each trial lasted approximately
9.0 seconds. Trials were separated by a five second
interval.
The 144 trials were delivered in three blocks of 48 trials
each with a three minute break between blocks. Each
block contained serves from one server, with equal
number of serves from both sides of the court and
equal number directed down the T and towards the
sidelines. Trials order was randomized with respect to
time of occlusion, side of court and direction of serve.
Blocks were counterbalanced across participants. On
average, each participant spent approximately 25
minutes to complete the experiment.
Serves were recorded from the returner’s perspective
with a digital video camera (Panasonic HC v770).
The centre of the camera lens was positioned on the
baseline at a height of 1.16 m and 0.74 m from the
corner of the court to create a representative viewing
perspective for WT serves. All footage was recorded
in high definition at 1080p resolution with 50 frames
per second. Servers were instructed to perform first
serves as they would in a match, directed towards
the centre of the court (down the ‘T’) and towards
the tramlines (‘out wide’) from each side of the court
(‘deuce’ and ‘advantage’). After filming, 16 ‘accurate’
serves, landing between 0 to 70 cm (Deshpande et al.,
in press) from the centre line or side line were selected
per server to create the familiarization and testing
trials.
The videos were imported into Adobe Premiere
Rush (Adobe Inc., 2022). The three servers exhibited
differences in the time required to complete the
serve. As advance information acquisition by experts
is linked to key temporal events and information
sources in the opponent’s action (Müller & Abernethy,
2012), individual serve videos were edited using
event-based temporal occlusion rather than occluding
at set time intervals. Based on serve biomechanics
determining serve direction in WT (D’Angeli, 2022),
previous research in AT (Jackson & Mogan, 2007),
and sources identified by professional WT players
(Deshpande et al., 2025), the starting position of the
server, ball trajectory to the apex of the ball toss, armracket orientation up to racket-ball contact, and early
ball flight were determined as potentially important
information sources for predicting serve direction in
WT. Hence, all clips were edited to occlude the video
at one of four time points (Table 2 and Figure 1).

Data collection
Testing of the skilled group took place in quiet office
spaces at the Nottingham Tennis Centre and the
National Tennis Centre, UK. Testing of the novice
group took place in quiet rooms at Loughborough
University. To facilitate data collection with precise
temporal control, responses were collected on a Dell
Precision M4800 laptop (Dell Technologies, United
States) using Experiment Center 3.7.69 software (SMI,
Germany). Gaze data was simultaneously collected
using the iView RED system (SMI, Germany) with the
sensor attached at the bottom of the monitor screen
in the centre. To create a representative view, the
trials were displayed on a 73 cm by 43 cm monitor,
with participants seated at a distance of 60 cm from
the screen. To confirm the representativeness of the
stimuli, a vertical visual angle was calculated for the
on-screen stimuli and returning serves on court. The
server image on screen subtended a vertical visual
angle of 2.67°, based on an average server image height
of 3 cm viewed from 60 cm. This closely approximates
the 2.82° visual angle experienced by WT returners on
court, given an average server height of 1.22 m and a
returner-to-server distance of 24.13 m along the court
diagonal.
Procedure
Participants were informed that they would be
presented with video clips of WT serves from the
returner’s perspective with equal number of serves
directed to the left or right. They were also informed
that clips would include videos occluded at four specific
points. They were asked to respond as quickly as
possible as if attempting to return the ball in an actual
match, by pressing the ‘z’ key for serves towards the
left and ‘?’ key for serves towards the right and make
their best possible guess when uncertain. Participants
started with 12 familiarization trials featuring
examples from each of the three servers with serves
on both sides of the court. They were instructed to
respond similar to the actual test instructions. Before
each block, participants completed a gaze calibration
procedure to ensure accurate eye-tracking.
Data analysis
BeGaze 3.7.80 (SMI, Germany) was used for calculating
response measures and visual behaviour measures.
The primary dependent variable was overall response
accuracy (RA) for each occlusion condition, which
was the percentage of correct responses for each
participant against each server and from each side of
the court.
Gaze behaviour data was analysed for the trials
occluded 200 ms after the server’s racket-ball contact.

Response accuracy and gaze behaviour data were

using z-scores. Shapiro-Wilk tests and Q-Q plots

degrees of freedom were used in case of violations.
Homogeneity of variances was tested and confirmed

Discussion
This study aimed to quantify expertise differences in
response accuracy and gaze behaviour while skilled
WT players and novices judged the direction of WT
serves using a video-based progressive temporal
occlusion approach. Based on prior findings in AT and
WT, it was hypothesised that the accuracy gap between
skilled players and novices would be greatest at the
earliest occlusion points and diminish as more visual
information became available, and that skilled players
would display fewer fixations of longer duration
compared to novices.
The primary hypothesis was rejected as ‘Expertise’
by Time of occlusion’ interaction for RA scores was
non-significant. Similar to Farrow et al. (2005), the
differences in RA scores was consistent for the first
three occlusions but eventually decreased for t4 (200
ms post racket-ball contact) with ceiling effects being
observed. This reflected similarity in the timing of
information pick up between the two groups, but
with better attunement to server’s evolving action
for skilled players than novices. The main effect of
‘Expertise’ is consistent with findings across multiple
sports demonstrating superior anticipatory skills in
experts compared to novices (Williams & Jackson,

Limitations
Two key limitations should be acknowledged.
First, while the video-based progressive temporal
occlusion approach afforded tight experimental
control, the button press response compromised task
representativeness in relation to the modality and
timing of participants' responses, thereby potentially
limiting ecological validity (Kredel et al., 2017). Future
studies should investigate serve perception in on-court
scenarios using larger displays, virtual reality stimuli,
or actual servers. Second, gaze behaviour was analysed
for the entire serve action rather than separately for
each phase. As the server's action unfolds, skilled
players may prioritise different information sources
in each phase of the action (Rosker & Rosker, 2021),
and future studies should examine gaze behaviour
separately for each biomechanical phase to better
understand this.
Conclusion
The current study explored the differences in
perception of WT serves between skilled WT players
and novices. Skilled WT players demonstrated superior
response accuracy compared to novices, confirming
existing literature on expertise-related differences
in anticipation across various sports. While skilled
WT players were able to accurately anticipate serve
direction in approximately two out of three serves,
their response accuracy was lower than that reported
for skilled AT players when the entire server’s action
up to racket-ball contact was available. Attunement
to information from the ball toss location was found
to be sub-optimal. Both skilled and novice groups
predominantly fixated on the predicted racket-ball
contact area, suggesting the use of this as an anchor
to pick up relevant information regarding the ball toss
and arm-racket movement.
