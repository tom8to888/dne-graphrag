Title: How to educate Coaches to work in Para Tennis

Introduction
A dedicated tennis coach can be the linchpin of
club life, maintaining contact both with the active
members they coach and, in the other direction, with
the club's executive board and external bodies such
as the regional and national tennis association. Given
that the quality and variety of a coach's programmes
considerably influence the club's public image and the
broader development of the sport, continuing education
for this group is crucial (Cushion et al., 2003; Gilbert
et al., 2006; Werthner & Trudel, 2006) – presumably
even more so for niche topics such as the structural
promotion of inclusion in sport and the participation
and performance of Para Tennis players.

Abstract
Coaches play one of the most important roles in sport and act as catalysts for new initiatives and the
development of new programmes. To enable them to work with athletes with disabilities, the German
Tennis Federation and the ‘Tennis for All’-Project of Gold-Kraemer-Foundation designed a coaches
education module for licensed tennis coaches, comprising 15 teaching units on the topics of inclusion,
managing diversity and specific rules, equipment and methodology. All five Para Tennis disciplines are
covered – Wheelchair Tennis, Blind and Visually Impaired Tennis, Para Standing Tennis, Deaf Tennis
and Learning Disability Tennis. The research project focused on two questions: How do participants
rate the general format and content of the course? Do participants feel that the course has given them
long-term ability to offer inclusive or disability-specific tennis training? Four of these courses, with a
total number of 56 participants, have been surveyed with two questionnaires; a first measurement took
place directly at the end of the course and a second one half a year later. The research findings indicate
a high level of satisfaction with the combination of theoretical background knowledge with practical
hands-on units and self-experience, such as using sports wheelchairs or Blind Tennis balls. Over 90%
of the participants self-report to feel able, following the course, to organise tennis training for players
with disabilities. Eight out of 56 coaches launched Para Tennis training programmes at their clubs within
the first six month after taking part.
Key words: Coaches Education; Inclusion; Para Tennis; Tennis Coaching.

A scoping review (Shaikh et al., 2021) underscores the
value of specific, parasport-related further training
programmes for both already active and prospective
coaches. Townsend, Huntley, Cushion and Culver
(2021) similarly argue that, in order to ensure that
para-athletes have access to sport and high-quality
sporting experiences, there is a need – and an ongoing
challenge in sports politics – to design and implement
the optimal structure for coach education, given that
such programmes are under-represented. Additionally,
these courses are largely not accompanied by scientific
research. This project is as a structured evaluation of
a specific continuing education module for tennis
coaches on teaching the Para Tennis disciplines and
thus represents an effort to address this general
research gap through a controlled efficacy study.

Two questionnaires, measurement M1 and M2, were
developed. M1 consists of a total of 16 close-ended
and two open-ended questions, initially to collect
demographic information and background data on
previous experience, subsequent to assess the course,
using a 6-point scale ranging from ‘I fully agree’ to
‘I don’t agree at all’ as well as ‘I don’t know’ and ‘No
answer’.  This design of the Likert scale was chosen to
avoid regression to the mean and to show more clearly
the direction in which respondents tend to lean (Chang,
1994). The questionnaire for M2 consists of a total
of nine close-ended and two open-ended questions.
Questions one to four relate to experiences since the
training course and can be answered with the options
‘yes’ or ‘no’, ‘I don’t know’ or ‘No answer’. Furthermore,
participants can elaborate on their experiences in
relation to the individual questions. Questions five to
nine deal with the effects of the coach education, with
the same 6-point response scale as in M1. Finally, there
are two open-ended questions designed to identify
wishes regarding the course and the implementation of
set objectives. Both questionnaires were pre-tested and
further refined based on feedback from experts. The
form for M1 was handed out to the participants printed
and completed directly after the course, using the
paper-and-pencil-method (Lucia et al., 2007). Everyone
was required to sign a consent form and generate
a personalised code so that both M1 and M2 could
be compared at the end and a progression of results
could be presented in a targeted manner. Six months
after each course, M2 was conducted via a sent link to
the accessible questionnaire tool ‘Unipark’ by E-Mail,
with a reminder after two weeks (Caspers, 2011). M1
was manually entered, M2 could be exported into
SPSS. Closed questions were analysed quantitatively
by carrying out a statistical analysis. The open-ended
questions were analysed qualitatively. Categories were
developed for this purpose so the responses could be
grouped, and main categories be defined based on
the frequency of mentions. All open-ended questions
regarding wishes and goals were analysed using both
qualitative content analysis and quantitative methods
(Mayring, 2015; 2016).
Various statistical methods were applied to survey
the quantitative results. The statements assessing
the course to be evaluated scalarly in M1 (e.g. ‘The
selection of content was thematically appropriate’)
and M2 (e.g. ‘The coaches education prepared me for
my role as a Coach in inclusive tennis’) were analysed
using descriptive statistics, also the median, minimum,
maximum and standard deviation were calculated.

As the participants held different licences, the
Mann-Whitney U test was used to examine whether
the responses to the training course evaluation (12
questions) differed between licence holders A and
B and those with licence C. The distribution of the
licence groups showed no significant differences in M1
when compared across all 12 questions. Similarly, the
results of M2, when comparing the licence types across
the respective five questions assessing the training
course, showed no differences. However, there was a
trend towards significance (p = .059) for the statement
in M2 ‘The course prepared me for my role as a coach
in inclusive and/or Para Tennis’ as holders of a higher
licence agree more with the statement (MRank = 6.00)
than holders of a lower licence (MRank = 10.00).
To examine correlations between the question one to
four ‘The course prepared me for my role as a coach in
inclusive and/or Para Tennis.’ (M1) and the question
‘Have you continued to engage with the topic in
your coaching practice following the course?’ (M2),
a correlation was calculated using Phi/Cramers’ V.
The results of the calculation are not significant (p =
0.38), meaning that there is no statistical correlation
at this point. In addition, the correlations between the
questions ‘The course prepared me for my role as a
coach in inclusive and/or Para Tennis.’  (M1) and ‘Have
you continued to engage with the theoretical aspects
of the course in your coaching practice following the
course?’ (M2) were also examined by calculating the
correlation using Phi/Cramér’s V and is likewise not
significant (p = .227). Following the same principle,
the two questions from the second measurement
point, ‘Have you continued to engage with the topic
in your coaching practice following the course?’ and
‘Have you continued to explore the theoretical aspects
of the training in more depth following the course?’,
were also analysed. The correlation calculation using
Phi/Cramér’s V was not significant (p = .302) as well.
At the first and second measurement points,
participants in the course were asked to rate the extent
to which they agreed with the statement: ‘The course
prepared me for my role as a coach in inclusive or Para
Tennis training’. The ratings of 14 participants could be
compared with one another. A comparison of the ranks
shows that there are five positive ranks, seven negative
ranks and two ties. Consequently, in five cases, the
assessment of the statement was better at the second
measurement point than at the first, and in seven cases
it was worse. In two cases, the assessment remained
the same. The Wilcoxon test was used to examine
whether the assessments of the statement differed
significantly between the two measurement points.
The p-value of p = .614 indicates that the assessment
of this statement does not differ significantly between
the two measurement points. Another statement on
which the participants were asked to comment at
both measurement points is: ‘Thanks to the course,
I feel able to work with special equipment such as
sports wheelchairs and Blind Tennis balls.’ To test
whether there were differences in the assessment of
this statement between the measurement points, the
Wilcoxon test was carried out again. The sample size
was N=14. Seven participants rated the statement
the same at both measurement points; four rated it
more favourably at the first measurement point; and
three rated the course more favourably at the second
measurement point. The Wilcoxon test confirms that
there is no significant difference between the two
measurement points (p=0.263).
In addition, participants had the opportunity to
formulate specific further requests via an openended questionnaire, which approximately half
of the participants took advantage of. These were
categorised, enabling five main categories to be
defined for both measurement times:
•	 Content
•	 Support
•	 Implementation
•	 Organisation
•	 Framework conditions
At the end of course M1, 14 people expressed a desire
for an even greater proportion of practical work
within the ‘Content’ category, whilst five people
requested more specific practical content relating
to club accessibility or learning disability tennis.
Related to this, there were calls for even more
training demonstrations and a wider range of detailed
presentations on specific training drills. Six months
later, at M2, the most frequent request was for more
practical work. In addition, participants called for
more information on training programme design,
guidance on entering competitions, and the possibility
of an integration of disabled athletes directly during
the course, additionally to the self-experience.
The ‘Support’ category, based on M1, covers assistance
with community integration, support from clubs and
federations, and the desire for financial support for
participation in further courses. At M2, there was a
renewed call for financial support through grants, as
well as for materials and campaigns from the regional
tennis associations.
On the topic of implementation, in M1 participants
also asked whether it would be possible to implement
practical trials with the target group as part of the
education. At M2, requests were made for workshadowing
opportunities
with
the
instructor,
further practical trials in small groups and ongoing
preparation for an implementation within a club.
Regarding organisation, the participation of people
with disabilities was highlighted during M1. It was
also called for more regular coaches education on
the topic of inclusion during the standard licensing
system in Germany, easier access to materials and
greater networking between Para Tennis coaches. Six
months later (M2), participants expressed a desire for
digital access to the theoretical sections. In addition,
they requested more materials for follow-up work,
more regular updates and a more intensive learning
experience.
Under the final main heading ‘Framework conditions’
for M1, comments were made regarding a longer
duration and the organisation of breaks. At M2, the
desire for a longer duration remained. Regarding
the question of objectives following the course,
the responses (from 54 people) were again divided
into various main categories. The ‘practical/active
implementation’ category includes the following
objectives: (further) implementing inclusion within
the club, encouraging people with disabilities to take
up tennis, gaining practical experience, considering
the implementation of a tennis training for players
with disabilities, taking on a part-time role as a tennis
coach, concrete offering Wheelchair Tennis at the
club, and coaching school classes with pupils with
and without disabilities together. Further objectives,
summarised under the heading ‘organisation’ include
raising awareness of inclusion within the club,
expanding networks, and engaging the club executives
with the issue and raising their awareness. Another
objective is to publicise inclusive programmes, identify
and remove barriers, and reach out to special needs
schools.  In addition, objectives relating to events were
set, e.g. ‘come and try’ days. Finally, personal goals of
the participants were also discussed. The aim is to
gain practical experience to contribute personally to
sport for people with disabilities, to undertake further
trainings, and to acquire training equipment.
At M2, eight people reported, in response to the
question about the objectives implemented, that
they had implemented them. This related to a tennis
training session that had taken place, an inclusive
try out day, introducing Para Tennis groups within
the club, planning Para Tennis training sessions,
and improving accessibility at the club. Four people
reported that they had not yet been able to implement
anything due to a lack of support from the club or the
regional association.
Finally, the questionnaire for M2 was expanded to
include an open-ended question on the need for
further information material. The following requests
were made:
•	 Coaching resources detailing training design,
exercises, more examples and suggestions
•	 Practical instructional video-tutorial or more
written online material, separated by specific
Para Tennis disciplines or medical conditions of
participants
In summary, the quantitative and qualitative analysis
yields the following results:
•	 Overall, the ‘Putting Inclusion into Tennis
practice’ coaches education module was very
well received by participants in terms of both
the choice of content and the structure of the
theoretical and practical components
•	 Over 96% of participants agree with the statement,
‘Overall, I enjoyed the course’
•	 Over 92% feel able, following the course, to
organise basic tennis training for athletes with
disabilities; over 98% feel able to implement
inclusive processes within their club
•	 In 68 out of 73 questionnaires received,
participants agreed with the statement: “The
content of the training has also had a positive
effect on my overall professionalism as a coach.”
•	 17 out of 56 participants also completed the
questionnaire six months after the course. Half of
them had initiated inclusive training programmes
by that point
•	 The participating coaches would like to see more
training and further education opportunities, as
well as a wide range of information materials on
the topic of coaching athletes with disabilities
Discussion
As the participants were given a questionnaire
to complete using the paper-and-pencil method
immediately after the course, and were given
sufficient time to answer it, a complete data collection
was recorded in M1. For M2, participants were
contacted by E-Mail after six months and sent a link
to the questionnaire. Despite a reminder E-Mail, the
response rate was low and the sample size small.
A study by Kordts-Freudinger and Geithner (2011)
shows that an online survey reaches fewer evaluation
participants than a direct paper-based survey.
The findings relating to the main research question
“How do participants rate the format and content of
the training course?” show high levels of satisfaction,
as the majority of responses to the questionnaire
were ‘fully agree’ and ‘agree’. Regarding the wishes
expressed, there is a desire for even more practical
content. In particular, the feedback from M2 indicated
a need for even more case studies and specific
information on training design. It can be assumed
that, whilst attempting to put what they had learnt
into practice afterwards, the participants realised
uncertainties in some areas. This is consistent with
findings that coaches mainly learn most effectively
through practical doing and hands-on self-experience
(Cushion, 2003), as well as through peer support and
mentoring (Gilbert et al., 2006).
The question was also raised, if it would be possible
to work with the target group, tennis players with
disabilities, during the course itself, e.g. to conduct a
specific Wheelchair Tennis training session with active
players, which also refers to the approach of educating
coaches directly in their practice environment. As
the module puts a focus on self-experiencing the
equipment, it does include practical experience using
sports wheelchairs or wearing blindfolds as part of its
practical content. Inviting athletes with disabilities
facilitates such encounters, though at the expense of
greater preparatory complexity and potentially higher
costs.
The participants goals following the course mainly
related to a desire to bring change within their own
clubs, to address the issue of inclusion there, and to
apply the knowledge they had newly acquired. Eight
people (half of the participants in M2) implemented
that goal within six months. Some other participants
were keen to develop inclusion but received no support
from clubs. In such cases, it may be helpful if concrete
support for the clubs is made available at the level of
the regional associations. This would ensure that a
club is not left to face major changes on its own and
receive support, which can further encourage those
who are often volunteers.
The low rate of direct implementation in the first
half of the year may be due to the sheer number and
complexity of the tasks faced by a club coach. As a
coach, setting up completely new programmes and
establishing contact with new target groups takes
(unpaid) time and effort. Another hypothesis for the
low response rate to M2 could reflect a certain sense of
embarrassment among participants about admitting
– to themselves and to the researchers through the
evaluation – that they had not yet put the course
content into practice during the first six months.
Consequently, they avoided this by not participating
in M2. Hypothetically, therefore, it takes more than
half a year or more than just one course or even
more external support mechanisms to implement
new topics into practical settings. This aligns with
the desire for ongoing engagement with the material
and exchange within experts and a coaches’ network,
even if it is only continued in digital form. A further
structured follow-up with all participants one or two
years after the course could prove worthwhile. A
larger longitudinal sample would generally increase
the validity of the results, so it is recommended that
additional courses will be evaluated. To avoid relying
only on self-reported measures of competence and
implementation, complementary methods such as
interviews with participants and experts should be
considered. Quantitative indicators – such as the
number of participating athletes with disabilities or
the number of Para Tennis lessons in the clubs – could
provide an additional measure of effectiveness.

As the way coaches learn can sometimes be kind of
idiosyncratic (Werthner & Trudel 2006), this research
highlights the importance of a well-structured
education programme and high-quality learning
materials, as well as a delivery by a skilled instructor.
The fact that the coaches are becoming aware of their
important role and signed up for the course on their
own initiative should already be seen as a positive first
step towards more inclusion.
Conclusion
The coach education module "Putting Inclusion into
Tennis Practice" developed jointly by the German
Tennis Federation (DTB) and the Gold-KraemerFoundation's "Tennis for All" project, was very
positively received by participants across all licence
levels, both in content and delivery. Particularly
well received were the balance between theory and
practice and the training in Para Tennis specific
methodology and equipment, although participants
expressed a desire for further practical examples and
direct try-outs with para-athletes during the course.
Participants subsequently feel confident to reach out
to new target groups, design training for players with
disabilities, and promote inclusive practices within
their clubs. There was also widespread agreement
that the module enhances participants' overall
professionalism as tennis coaches more broadly.
Participants further expressed interest in additional
qualification
opportunities,
including
follow-up
courses, work-shadowing with experienced colleagues,
and illustrative materials to support their ongoing
professional development. The scientific evaluation
of the format contributes to its professionalisation
and may support an increase in the number of such
courses offered – a development that could positively
affect reducing barriers in tennis. Designed to prepare
licensed coaches to work with athletes across all five
Para Tennis disciplines, this novel module addresses
an important area of coach development under the
responsibility of the national association. The high
levels of participant satisfaction and increased selfreported confidence reported here suggest a promising
foundation for strengthening this field in Germany
over the long term.
The concept of this module could be implemented
in other countries and tennis associations with
minimal effort. It also seems worthwhile to adapt this
approach and the research findings to other sports.
Ultimately, coaches' confidence in parasport-specific
methodology
and
equipment,
fostered
through
structured education, is essential for promoting the
participation of people with disabilities in sport and
in society alike.
