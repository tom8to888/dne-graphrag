Abstract
Wheelchair tennis is an important para sport that supports the participation of individuals with
disabilities in tennis and has evolved from a rehabilitation-oriented activity into an international discipline
requiring high-level physical, technical, tactical, and cognitive performance. This review examines
the historical emergence of wheelchair tennis, the pioneering role of Brad Parks, the international
institutionalization of the sport, its basic game structure, current profile in Türkiye, and key coaching
implications. Literature and official institutional sources indicate that wheelchair tennis emerged in
the United States in 1976, gained an international federation structure in 1988, became a full-medal
sport at the Barcelona 1992 Paralympic Games, and was fully integrated into the International Tennis
Federation in 1998. The review also highlights the two-bounce rule, classification structure, wheelchair
mobility demands, and the interaction between racket use and wheelchair configuration as distinctive
features of the sport. In Türkiye, recent national competitions, Paralympic participation, and Ahmet
Kaplan’s historic Roland-Garros performance demonstrate the sport’s growing development potential.
Sustainable progress requires accessible facilities, structured coach education, evidence-informed
training, early introductory pathways, and long-term athlete-development models.
Key words: wheelchair tennis; para sport; tennis coaching; paralympic sport.

Brad Parks’ contribution extended beyond the initial

Game structure, rules, and classification
Wheelchair tennis is closely aligned with conventional
tennis in terms of its basic game structure and
equipment. Court dimensions, rackets, and balls are
the same as those used in the conventional game,
which helps preserve continuity with the wider tennis
environment. The most distinctive rule modification
is the “two-bounce rule.” According to the ITF
wheelchair tennis regulations, a player may allow the
ball to bounce twice before returning it, but the ball
must be returned before a third bounce. The second
bounce may occur either inside or outside the court
boundaries (International Tennis Federation [ITF],
2026a). This adaptation responds to the specific
mobility demands of wheelchair play while preserving
the tactical and competitive logic of tennis.
Classification is another central component of
wheelchair tennis because it aims to support fair and
meaningful competition among athletes with different
functional profiles. The ITF classification system
identifies two main sport classes: the Open Division
and the Quad Division. The Open Division includes
athletes with a permanent physical impairment
affecting one or both lower extremities that impacts
tennis performance. The Quad Division includes
athletes whose impairments also affect the upper
extremities and involve functional limitations in at
least three limbs (ITF, 2019). In terms of competition
structure, male and female athletes compete in
separate Open Division draws, whereas male and
female athletes in the Quad Division compete
together in one draw. This classification approach is
designed to ensure that competitive outcomes are
determined as much as possible by tennis ability,
Wheelchair tennis represents one of the most
visible examples of this historical and sporting
transformation.

Historical emergence and the role of
brad parks
The
modern
emergence
of
is primarily associated with Brad Parks, whose
pioneering efforts transformed the sport from a
rehabilitation-oriented activity into an organized
competitive discipline. After becoming paraplegic
following a freestyle skiing accident at the age of 18 in
1976, Parks began experimenting with playing tennis
from a wheelchair during his rehabilitation. This
period marked the beginning of wheelchair tennis as
more than a therapeutic exercise, as Parks, together
with fellow wheelchair athlete Jeff Minnebraker,
recognized the potential of the sport as a structured
and competitive form of tennis (International
Paralympic Committee [IPC], 2012; International
Tennis Federation [ITF], 2020).
The late 1970s and early 1980s were crucial for the
early institutional development of the sport. Parks
and Minnebraker promoted wheelchair tennis through
camps and exhibition activities, helping to increase
awareness and participation. The Los Angeles City and
Recreation Department’s organization of one of the
first wheelchair tennis tournaments in 1977 signaled
the beginning of formal competitive opportunities.
In 1980, Parks joined David Saltz, Jim Worth, and
Dave Kiley to establish the National Foundation
of Wheelchair Tennis. This organization created
a 10-tournament circuit across the United States,
culminating in a national championship that attracted
more than 70 players and demonstrated the sport’s
rapid growth and early organizational consolidation
(IPC, 2012).

Today, wheelchair tennis operates a professional

Importance for sport science and
coaching
Wheelchair tennis is a multifaceted sport that extends
beyond a simple adaptation of conventional tennis.
It requires the integrated development of physical,
technical, tactical, and cognitive skills. Athletes
must control the wheelchair through propulsion,
acceleration, braking, turning, and repositioning
while
simultaneously
preparing
executing
racket strokes. This dual-task requirement places
considerable demands on trunk control, balance,
upper-extremity strength, coordination, anticipation,
and tactical decision-making, all of which are central
to performance in wheelchair tennis.
Rietveld et al. (2024) emphasize that wheelchair tennis
performance is shaped by the interaction among
player characteristics, wheelchair configuration, and
the playing environment. The need to coordinate
wheelchair propulsion with racket handling creates a
distinctive performance context compared with other
wheelchair court sports. For this reason, coaching
should not focus solely on stroke mechanics, but should
integrate wheelchair mobility, court positioning,
recovery movements, and tactical decision-making.
Coaches should consider how athletes approach the
contact point, orient the wheelchair in relation to the
ball, rotate during stroke preparation, and recover
position after each shot.
The need for a sport-science-based coaching approach
is also supported by earlier reviews of wheelchair
tennis research. Villaplana Velasco and Fuentes García
(2015) reviewed sport science publications specific to
wheelchair tennis and concluded that further research
is needed in physiology, motor control and learning,
biomechanics, tactical analysis, psychology, and
sociology. This indicates that coaching in wheelchair
tennis should not rely only on general tennis
knowledge, but should be informed by disciplinespecific evidence related to wheelchair mobility,
stroke production, physical conditioning, perceptual
skills, and the psychosocial demands of the sport.

A further rule-specific feature is that the wheelchair
is considered part of the player’s body. Therefore, all
applicable rules that apply to the player’s body also
apply to the wheelchair (ITF, 2026a). This principle
is important for interpreting ball contact, movement,
boundaries, and faults during play. In practice, the
interaction between the player, wheelchair, and
racket gives wheelchair tennis a distinctive technical
profile. Sports wheelchairs are designed to support
acceleration, braking, turning, stability, and court
coverage, and their configuration can influence
movement efficiency and performance. Therefore, the
rules and classification structure of wheelchair tennis
should be understood not only as formal regulations
but also as key elements that shape coaching, tactical
planning, and athlete development (Rietveld et al.,
2024).
Global
institutionalization
professionalization
From the 1980s onward, wheelchair tennis began
to expand beyond its origins in the United States,
marking an important shift toward international
recognition and institutional development. A key
milestone was its inclusion in the Stoke Mandeville
Wheelchair Games in 1987, which provided an early
platform for international competition and visibility.
The establishment of the International Wheelchair
Tennis Federation (IWTF) in 1988 created a formal
governance structure dedicated to the sport and
supported its development beyond national contexts
(International Paralympic Committee [IPC], 2012).
The Paralympic movement played a decisive role in
increasing the global profile of wheelchair tennis.
The sport was included as a demonstration event at
the 1988 Seoul Paralympic Games and became a fullmedal sport at the 1992 Barcelona Paralympic Games.
Brad Parks and Randy Snow’s gold medal in men’s
doubles at Barcelona symbolized this transition at the
athlete level and reflected the sport’s emergence as
an elite competitive discipline within the Paralympic
programme (IPC, 2012).
Institutionally, the full integration of the IWTF into
the International Tennis Federation (ITF) in 1998
represented a major turning point. This integration
placed
within
the
broader
international governance structure of tennis and
strengthened its connection with the wider tennis
system. In the following years, wheelchair tennis
continued to expand at the highest levels of the sport.
Wheelchair tennis made its Grand Slam debut with a
full competitive draw at the Australian Open in 2002,
and by 2007 all four Grand Slam tournaments included
Tennis Championships. Competing in the Quad
Division, Kaplan reached the final after defeating
the tournament’s top-seeded opponent in the semifinal, thereby becoming the first Turkish athlete to
reach a Grand Slam singles final in wheelchair tennis
(Turkish Tennis Federation [TTF], 2026a; RolandGarros, 2026a). Although he was defeated in the final,
his runner-up finish represents a historic milestone
for the international visibility of wheelchair tennis in
Türkiye (Roland-Garros, 2026b; TTF, 2026b).
Kaplan’s achievement is important not only as
an individual performance result but also as a
developmental indicator of the potential of wheelchair
tennis in Türkiye. At the same time, it draws attention
to the relatively narrow pool of Turkish athletes
capable of sustaining performance at the highest
international level in this discipline. In this sense,
Kaplan’s Roland-Garros final highlights the need to
strengthen athlete-development pathways, coach
education, classification knowledge, international
tournament experience, and sustainable support
systems. His success also provides a powerful national
role model, which may help increase the visibility
of wheelchair tennis, expand the athlete base, and
encourage greater institutional support for the sport
in Türkiye.
Recommendations for development
For wheelchair tennis to develop sustainably in Türkiye,
local and regional development centers should be
established to improve access, training opportunities,
and athlete-development pathways. Accessibility
standards in tennis clubs should also be strengthened
so that wheelchair tennis can be practiced in inclusive
and safe environments. In addition, wheelchairtennis-specific modules should be integrated into
coach education programs to provide coaches with the
technical, tactical, classification-related, and injuryprevention knowledge required in this discipline.
Strategic cooperation among sport sciences faculties,
municipalities, tennis clubs, federations, disability
organizations, rehabilitation centers, and the private
sector may expand grassroots participation while also
supporting athletes at the performance level. From
an athlete-development perspective, a long-term
athlete development model tailored to wheelchair
tennis should include introductory, developmental,
and performance stages. With appropriate guidance,
medically
informed
support,
age-sensitive
introductory programs, entry into wheelchair tennis
in Türkiye may be encouraged at younger ages.
Increasing local tournament opportunities, improving
equipment and transportation support, and developing
targeted programs for women, youth athletes, and
Quad Division athletes may further strengthen the
sport’s national development.
Training programs should be individualized according
to the athlete’s classification status, functional
capacity,
biomechanical
characteristics,
upperextremity
loading
patterns,
set-up,
and injury-prevention needs. For example, seating
position, wheel camber, and chair stability may
influence mobility efficiency and stroke execution. At
the same time, conditioning programs should address
muscular
endurance,
shoulder
upper-limb
health, trunk stability, and repeated high-intensity
movement demands. Psychological preparation is
also important, as athletes must maintain focus,
confidence, motivation, and decision-making quality
under competitive pressure.
Accordingly, the development of wheelchair tennis
depends not only on increasing the number and
quality of competitions, but also on strengthening the
scientific foundation of training, injury prevention,
athlete development, and coaching practice. Such an
approach may contribute to improved performance
outcomes, safer participation, and the sustainable
growth of wheelchair tennis both internationally and
in Türkiye.
Current profile of wheelchair tennis in
türki̇ye
The current profile of wheelchair tennis in Türkiye
reflects a developing para sport supported by the
Turkish Tennis Federation and the Turkish National
Paralympic Committee. According to the Turkish
National Paralympic Committee, wheelchair tennis
is characterized by its strong compatibility with
conventional tennis and includes men’s, women’s, and
quad categories in both singles and doubles formats
(Türkiye Milli Paralimpik Komitesi [TMPK], n.d.).
In recent years, the visibility of wheelchair tennis in
Türkiye has increased through national competitions,
international
tournament
participation,
the organization of ITF-sanctioned events. The
participation of Ahmet Kaplan and Uğur Altınel
in the Quad Division at the Paris 2024 Paralympic
Games represents an important indicator of Türkiye’s
growing presence in international wheelchair tennis.
Kaplan’s progression to the singles quarterfinals at
Paris 2024 further demonstrates the competitive
potential of Turkish athletes in this discipline (Türkiye
Tenis Federasyonu [TTF], 2024). Nevertheless, the
development of wheelchair tennis in Türkiye still
requires continued strategic support in areas such
as accessible facilities, coach and referee education,
athlete classification, talent identification, local club
structures, and regular tournament opportunities.

In conclusion, wheelchair tennis is a dynamic para
sport that bridges rehabilitation, recreation, and elite
competition. Its continued advancement in Türkiye
