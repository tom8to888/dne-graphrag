Introduction
Tennis schedules can expose players to prolonged
intermittent exercise in hot and humid conditions.
Environmental heat strain can impair performance
and, in severe cases, contribute to exertional heat
illness. Contemporary guidance therefore treats
heat management as a shared responsibility among
athletes, coaches, medical personnel, officials, and
tournament organisers rather than as a matter of
hydration alone (Pluim et al., 2026; Racinais et al.,
2023). This is particularly important in wheelchair
tennis, where tennis-specific demands interact with
heterogeneous impairments, wheelchair propulsion,
and potentially altered thermoregulatory function.

Abstract
Heat-risk management in wheelchair tennis requires coordination between tournament procedures
and athlete-specific assessment, but rules and evidence address different parts of the problem. This
targeted narrative review combined PubMed searches, reference tracing, wheelchair-tennis literature,
broader spinal cord injury (SCI) and Para sport evidence, general tennis research, and current ITF
regulations. Direct evidence was sparse and based mainly on small observational or crossover studies.
Cooling generally reduced physiological or perceptual thermal strain more consistently than improved
performance. Broader evidence indicated variation associated with thermoregulatory function, lesion
characteristics, previous heat response, health status, acclimation, hydration, and cooling access. The
2026 ITF regulations provide tournament-level environmental triggers and procedures for modifying or
suspending play, but they do not define individual medical safety. The proposed framework therefore
separates tournament-level triggers from athlete-specific risk and assigns time-sequenced actions to
players, coaches, officials, tournament organisers, and medical personnel. It distinguishes rule-based
requirements, evidence-informed recommendations, conditional practical considerations, and medical
escalation. The framework supports transparent decision-making but is not a validated predictor of
safety or performance.

Methods
Design and scope
This article is a targeted narrative review with
evidence-informed practical framework synthesis.
This design was selected because the objective was
to translate a small and heterogeneous evidence
base, together with current competition rules, into
role-specific actions rather than to estimate a pooled
intervention effect or claim exhaustive coverage.
The article is therefore not presented as a systematic
review or meta-analysis.
The review considered four source groups: wheelchairtennis-specific studies; research involving athletes or
physically active people with SCI or closely related
impairments; transferable general tennis and Para
sport evidence; and current regulations, consensus
statements, and official health-care guidance.
Literature identification
Targeted PubMed searches were conducted on
3 August 2026 using combinations of the terms
“wheelchair tennis”, “heat”, “thermoregulation”,
“cooling”, “hydration”, “spinal cord injury”, “Para
athlete”, “tennis”, and “wet-bulb globe temperature”.
Key wheelchair-tennis and SCI reviews were used
for backward citation tracing, and related PubMed
records were checked for more recent studies. The
WTCSSR archive and the official ITF and IOC websites
were searched for tennis-specific reviews, current
regulations, and health-care guidance.
Sources were included when they informed heat
strain or thermoregulatory function; impairment- or
athlete-related risk modifiers; preparation, hydration,
cooling,
symptom
recognition,
withdrawal,
or

procedures; or stakeholder roles and authority.
Commercial or unverifiable material was excluded.
Evidence from adjacent populations was used only
when the transfer pathway to wheelchair tennis could
be stated explicitly.

integrate evidence on heat strain, thermoregulatory
impairment, and heat mitigation with the 2026 ITF

decision framework for wheelchair tennis. The

for

officials,

Evidence base identified by targeted searching
Targeted PubMed searching and citation tracing

single-athlete case report involving a similar cooling
approach, an eight-player field pilot, an ice-slurry

Framework development
Evidence was organised by population directness:
direct wheelchair-tennis evidence; SCI or closely
related wheelchair-sport evidence; other Para sport or
general tennis evidence; and authoritative regulations
or guidance. Actions were then classified as rule-based
requirements, evidence-informed recommendations,
conditional practical considerations, or medical
assessment and escalation. Regulatory authority was
not treated as proof of physiological effectiveness,
and scientific evidence was not treated as conferring
tournament-wide decision authority.
The synthesis mapped tournament-level triggers,
athlete-specific risk modifiers, mitigation options,
and role-specific actions across competition phases.
No meta-analysis or statistical vote counting was
performed. Actions were included only when they could
be traced to a rule, a scientific source, an authoritative
guideline, or an explicitly labelled practical inference.

These provisions are operational triggers, not
individual medical safety thresholds. SCI syntheses
show that thermal strain varies with neurological level,
completeness, preserved sweating capacity and surface
area, and autonomic function. Grossmann et al. (2021)
reviewed 32 studies and found the greatest burden
among participants with higher lesions or tetraplegia.
O'Brien et al. (2022) found that cooling reduced core
temperature, skin temperature, and thermal sensation
overall, with larger effects at higher lesion levels. The
evidence therefore supports functional and athletespecific assessment rather than assumptions based on
wheelchair use or diagnosis alone.
Mitigation and framework synthesis
The evidence did not support cooling as a substitute
for hydration, preparation, symptom monitoring,
or regulatory action. Broader Para sport evidence
suggested potential roles for acclimation, pre-cooling,
per-cooling, and skin wetting in selected athletes but
also showed heterogeneous responses. Among 107
Paralympic athletes, 57% reported previous heatstress symptoms and 9% reported medically diagnosed
exertional heat illness; heat acclimation and cooling
were not universally used (Alkemade, Daanen, et
al., 2023). General tennis data likewise suggested
differences in access to and use of strategies according
to competitive level and environment (Robin et al.,
2024).
The framework retained two non-interchangeable
layers. The tournament layer comprises environmental
measurement, communication, schedule modification,
breaks, suspension, and required resources. The
athlete layer comprises previous heat response,
thermoregulatory function, health and medication
considerations,
acclimation,
hydration,
feasible
cooling, current symptoms, and access to medical
assessment. Tournament organisers and officials
establish and operate the event system; players,
coaches, and medical personnel establish individual
precautions and respond to deterioration. Individual
withdrawal or medical escalation may be necessary
below a tournament-wide threshold, but individual
concern does not alter the formal suspension authority
assigned by the regulations.
The synthesis supported three practical conclusions:
tournament-level triggers and athlete-specific risk
are both necessary but non-substitutable; practical
actions draw on evidence of differing directness; and
responsibilities are distributed across stakeholders and
phases while formal authority remains concentrated
in designated tournament and medical roles.

Wheelchair-tennis-specific evidence
In a crossover trial, eight highly trained wheelchairtennis players completed two 60-minute intermittent
sprint sessions at 30.4 ± 0.6°C, with and without headand-neck cooling (Goosey-Tolfrey et al., 2008). Fluid
intake was lower with cooling (700 ± 393 mL compared
with 1,198 ± 675 mL; p = .042). Thermal sensation
and perceived exertion were numerically lower with
cooling, but the differences were not statistically
significant. Participants were mildly dehydrated on
average, suggesting that improved thermal comfort
may not ensure adequate fluid intake.
The related case report of a world-class female player
with incomplete SCI illustrated the feasibility of
combining pre-cooling with head-and-neck cooling
during a simulated one-hour protocol. Thermal
sensation was lower and peak sprint speed was better
maintained, but the single-participant design and its
use of a similar cooling approach to the 2008 study
precluded treatment as independent confirmatory
evidence (Diaper & Goosey-Tolfrey, 2009; GooseyTolfrey et al., 2008). At the 2013 British Open,
Veltmeijer et al. (2014) monitored eight players in
moderate conditions (WBGT, 18–20°C). Usable coretemperature data were available for two players
with SCI and four without SCI; increases were 0.6 ±
0.1°C and 0.3 ± 0.1°C, respectively. The sample and
environmental exposure were insufficient to establish
a threshold or causal conclusion.
In a subsequent trial at approximately 32°C, ice-slurry
ingestion lowered gastrointestinal and forehead
temperatures, thermal sensation, and sweat rate but
did not improve repeated 20-m sprint performance
(Stephenson et al., 2022). Across the direct studies,
cooling effects were more consistent for physiological
or perceptual strain than for performance, and no
single superior match-play strategy was identified.
A mixed-sport study including wheelchair-tennis
and para-cycling athletes found that hot-humid
conditions reduced maximal exercise performance,
but the heterogeneous sports and impairments did not
permit a wheelchair-tennis-specific effect estimate
(Alkemade, de Korte, et al., 2023).

Implications for coaching and event practice
Responsibility is distributed but not symmetrical.
Players can report symptoms and stop play; coaches
can support preparation, monitoring, communication,
and withdrawal decisions. Tournament organisers
establish environmental monitoring, shade, ice,
scheduling, communication, and medical systems.
Officials apply the governing procedures and exercise
the authority assigned to them. Medical personnel
assess symptoms, treat suspected heat illness, and
advise on withdrawal or return to play. This structure
is consistent with the IOC emphasis on coordinated
planning (Racinais et al., 2023) and with ITF healthcare guidance recommending emergency planning,
water, ice, accessible treatment areas, and appropriate
medical expertise at wheelchair-tennis events (ITF,
2025).
For coaches, the practical task is not to diagnose
heat illness or make tournament-wide suspension
decisions. It is to know the athlete's heat history,
prepare permitted hydration and cooling options,
recognise meaningful deterioration, and facilitate
communication, withdrawal, and medical escalation.
The evidence codes reinforce these boundaries. A
rule-based requirement derives from governing
authority; an evidence-informed recommendation
derives from empirical or consensus evidence but
creates no competition authority; and a conditional
practical consideration remains dependent on indirect
evidence, context, and resources. Keeping these
categories separate reduces the risk of presenting a
governing threshold as a universal safety boundary or
a plausible cooling strategy as a mandatory standard.
Limitations and future research
The principal limitation is the targeted narrative
design. PubMed, citation tracing, WTCSSR articles,
and official documents were used, but the review did
not employ exhaustive multi-database searching,
independent dual screening, or formal risk-of-bias
scoring. Relevant literature may therefore have been
missed, and source selection and synthesis depended
on author judgement. The direct evidence involved
very small samples, and the cooling component of
the 2009 single-athlete report was not treated as
independent confirmation of the 2008 crossover
findings. Interpretation of the Veltmeijer et al.
(2014) pilot was subsequently debated in a published
Letter to the Editor (Silver, 2014), further supporting
cautious use of its small descriptive dataset. Evidence
disproportionately concerns SCI, with limited direct
information on other eligible impairments, women,
junior players, Quad players, doubles, non-hardcourt settings, and resource-constrained tournaments

Discussion
Principal findings and interpretation
This
targeted
synthesis
indicates
that
heatrisk management in wheelchair tennis is a twolayer decision problem. The 2026 ITF regulations
provide tournament-level environmental triggers,
communication procedures, and formal authority to
modify or suspend play. These provisions promote
consistent tournament management but are not
individual medical safety thresholds. Athlete-level
risk varies with thermoregulatory function, lesion
characteristics where SCI is present, previous heat
response, acclimation, hydration, health status,
clinically relevant medication considerations, and
feasible mitigation. By contrast, elevated individual
risk does not confer authority to suspend a tournament.
The framework therefore preserves the boundary
between competition governance and personalised
risk management.
The practical evidence base is less direct than many
apparently straightforward heat recommendations
imply. The wheelchair-tennis literature identified
through
targeted
searching
comprised
few,
heterogeneous reports based on very small samples,
and most assessed simulated exercise or physiological
and perceptual responses rather than heat illness,
match completion, or clinical outcomes. Cooling
generally reduced physiological or perceptual strain
without consistently improving performance. The
lower fluid intake observed during head-and-neck
cooling warrants caution because improved thermal
comfort may not reliably signal adequate hydration
(Goosey-Tolfrey et al., 2008). Cooling should therefore
be considered one component of preparation and
symptom management, not a substitute for hydration,
environmental monitoring, or escalation.
These findings are consistent with SCI evidence
suggesting greater thermal strain at higher lesion
levels and potential benefits of selected pre- or percooling strategies (Grossmann et al., 2021; O'Brien
et al., 2022). A mixed-sport study that included
wheelchair-tennis athletes further showed substantial
performance impairment in humid heat, although
its heterogeneous sample prevents tennis-specific
inference (Alkemade, de Korte, et al., 2023). Transfer
to wheelchair tennis nevertheless requires caution
because players have varied impairments and the
functional consequences of a diagnosis differ. A
function-based approach that considers sweating
capacity,
previous
heat
intolerance,
autonomic
dysfunction, health and medication considerations,
and cooling feasibility is more defensible than a
diagnosis-based rule.
