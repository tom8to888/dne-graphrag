In 2026 we celebrate 50 years of Wheelchair Tennis.
Five decades since Brad Parks first took to a Californian
tennis court following a spinal cord injury. Half a
century of growth has transformed Wheelchair Tennis
into a global sport with over 60 nations currently
represented on the World Tennis Wheelchair Rankings.
The game is played from local parks and tennis courts
to the world stage at the Paralympic games and the
four Grand Slams. For the Wheelchair Tennis team, it
is about the sport enhancing people’s lives, whether a
player, coach, or fan.
Milestones from the 50th
Over the 50 years of Wheelchair tennis there have been
multiple key milestones, from the first tournament in
1977 to the introduction of the Premier Tier in 2026,
the space afforded here does not allow us to cover all
of them, but to bring your attention to some that have
shaped the sport the most:
1988 – Introduction of the “two-bounce” rule. One
of the defining features of the sport and one of only
two rule differences with non-disabled tennis. The
other being that at least one  buttock needs to always
be in contact with the chair.
1992 – Inclusion in the Paralympic games. The
Paralympic Games are amongst the biggest sporting
events in the world; the Barcelona 1992 games saw
the sport included for the first time. The growth
and popularity of Wheelchair Tennis is perhaps best
illustrated through its Paralympic history; as we
reached the Men’s final at the Paris 2024 Paralympic
Games over 15,000 fans were packed into Philippe
Chatrier to witness a match with uncertainty to the
very last point (World Tennis, 2024).
2007 – Inclusion at all four Grand Slams. Wheelchair
tennis was first included as a demonstration event at
Wimbledon in 2001, and then for the first time as an
official event at the Australian Open in 2002. By 2007
all Grand Slams had wheelchair events, showcasing
the sport at the biggest stages in world tennis.

Coaching in Wheelchair Tennis
Given the focus of this of this journal, the importance
of coaches and coaching will come as no surprise
to those reading. Relying on research from across
Paralympic sport, we see coaching as amongst the
most important factors across an athlete’s entire
journey (Pankowiak et al., 2023; Patatas et al., 2018).
From introducing players to a sport for the first time,
through their ongoing development, to achieving
their performance goals, and eventually retiring from
sport, coaches play an important role.
As a wheelchair tennis team, we take the view that
tennis is tennis. A good tennis coach will be able to
adapt their practice to meet the needs of any player,
whether using a wheelchair or standing. However, we
are aware of the risks of unknowingly and uncritically
adopting practice from non-disabled sport. We are
also aware that lack of disability sport knowledge
may act as a barrier to coaches feeling able to support
wheelchair players and stigma around disability
may prevent a player seeking a coach (Wareham
et al., 2018). One approach we take to remedy this
