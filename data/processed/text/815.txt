Introduction
Wheelchair tennis has undergone a great evolution
since its appearance as an exhibition sport at the
Seoul 1988 Paralympic Games and after being part of
the official program in Barcelona 1992, suffering since
then one of the fastest growths of the Paralympic
program, with the presence of athletes from more than
a hundred countries and a progressive incorporation
into the four Grand Slam tournaments.  starting with
the Australian Open in 2002, followed by the US Open
(2005), Wimbledon (2005 doubles and 2016 singles) and
Roland Garros (2007). Recently, some ATP and WTA
tournaments have also begun to integrate wheelchair
tennis competitions into their own programmes, thanks
to the restructuring of the UNIQLO Wheelchair Tennis
Tour, which may make it a trend on the World Tennis
circuit itself (ITF, 2026).

Abstract
Wheelchair tennis has undergone a remarkable evolution over the last decades, both in participation
and professionalization as well as in integration within the professional tennis circuit (WT/ATP/WTA).
At the same time, the increase in the physical and technical-tactical demands of the game invites us
to analyse whether the functional profile of players capable of reaching the elite is also changing.
This article proposes, based on the scientific literature and documented cases, the hypothesis that the
increasing demands for mobility, acceleration, power and technical actions may progressively favour
athletes with lower functional deficits within the Open Division. Recent studies show that trunk function
conditions relevant mobility variables and that functional severity can affect actions such as service.
This evidence is analysed alongside the generational contrast between players such as Shingo Kunieda,
Alfie Hewett and Tokito Oda, whose characteristics illustrate the heterogeneity currently present in
the elite. A possible inclusion-exclusion paradox is thus posed: a sport that emerged as a competitive
sport suitable for people with a significant physical impairment could evolve towards high performance
in which certain profiles find it more difficult to reach the top positions. Finally, we propose a second
reflection on the relationship between the current simple ranking system and the successful integration
of wheelchair tennis in Grand Slams and professional tournaments. Both issues require longitudinal
analyses before drawing conclusions, but they open a relevant debate for classification, the recruitment
of new athletes and the future governance of wheelchair tennis.
Key words: wheelchair tennis; sports classification; spinal cord injury; sports performance.

It has also changed the game itself. The competition

the other hand, concentrates the competition in two
major divisions: Open and Quad.
The Open Division groups together athletes who have
sufficient functional loss in one or both lower limbs.
The Quad category requires additional involvement in
the upper extremities. Within the Open there are no
categories that differentiate players according to the
degree of function of the trunk, the type of injury or the
severity of its affectation (ITF, 2026).
This structure has obvious advantages. It facilitates
competition, simplifies the structure of the frames and
allows athletes with different physical disabilities to
face each other.
Contemporary Paralympic classification tries to ensure
that success depends mainly on one's abilities to
practice sport and not on the severity of the disability
(Tweedy & Vanlandewijck, 2011; Tweedy et al., 2014).
Achieving this goal requires knowing precisely which
bodily functions affect performance.
At wheelchair tennis there are still open questions.
Hutchinson et al. (2021) reviewed the measures
applicable to the classification of chair sports and
found limitations in the validity and reliability of
several procedures used to assess coordination, range
of motion, and other functions. The current rankings
make it possible to determine who can compete,
but there is less evidence on how much functional
differences influence the eligible players themselves.
One of the first works directly related to this issue was
that of Cavedon et al. (2014). When analysing players
grouped according to different levels of functional
limitation, they found significant differences in service
variables. Athletes with less impairment could generate
higher hitting speeds, which showed that functional
characteristics had a mechanical implication on a
relevant action of the game. This aspect becomes more
interesting as wheelchair tennis increases its demands.
The work on the analysis of the activity pattern has
described a progressive intensity in the gambling actions.
Between the Athens 2004 and Rio 2016 Paralympic
Games, different indicators related to duration, number
of strokes and point structure increased (Sánchez-Pay
and Sanz-Rivas, 2021a). Other studies have confirmed
significant physiological demands during competition
and differences according to level and playing surface
(Sindall et al., 2013; Sánchez-Pay et al., 2016; Ponzano
& Gollin, 2017; Sánchez-Pay & Sanz-Rivas, 2021b).
Williamson et al. (2024), in a systematic review with
meta-analysis, confirmed the high physical demand

However, given this development, we wonder if the
functional profile of the players who achieve the top
positions in the world ranking has also changed.
We do not yet have a longitudinal study that
allows us to answer precisely. The World Tennis'
historical ranking lists are not systematically linked
to clinical and functional information on players.
Even so, the observation of elite competition, some
well-documented cases and, above all, the recent
development of research, allow us to formulate a
hypothesis that we consider relevant.
Wheelchair tennis was born closely linked to people
with spinal cord injury and other serious impairments of
mobility. Currently, within the Open Division, athletes
with very different profiles compete, complete and
incomplete spinal cord injuries, amputations, sequelae
of bone tumours, hip alterations and other deficiencies
that meet the minimum disability criteria. All of them
are legitimately eligible and what we wonder is whether
the evolution of the game may be progressively
increasing the competitive value of certain residual
functional abilities. In other words, if trunk function,
acceleration and wheelchair mobility are becoming
increasingly decisive, it may be that players who retain
higher levels of these abilities have an advantage that
the current ranking system does not fully compensate.
This approach also takes on a social dimension. In many
countries, wheelchair tennis is introduced in hospitals
and rehabilitation centres as a sports option for people
who have just contracted a spinal cord injury. Elite
players are supposed to be role models and role models
in this process, but if the functional profile of those
who dominate the circuit progressively distances itself
from that of these new practitioners, the perception of
whether it is an attractive sport to practice or not could
also change.
We do not raise this reflection to question the
results, merits or right to compete of any athlete.
Everyone participates according to the established
eligibility rules. The interest is on another level: how
classification, performance evolution and the level of
functional impairment interact, and what consequences
this interaction may have for the future of wheelchair
tennis.
Functional classification and evolution
of the demands of the game
In wheelchair tennis, the classification system has
a characteristic that differentiates it from other
sports practiced in wheelchairs. In disciplines such as

reached the quarterfinals of the men's modality at the
Paralympic Games from Barcelona 1992 to Paris 2024.
For more than a decade, Shingo Kunieda was the
dominant figure in men's chair tennis. His career and
number of titles have placed him as one of the great
historical references of wheelchair tennis. Kunieda
presented paralysis of the lower limbs because of
a spinal tumour, a profile clearly associated with a
severe neurological impairment of mobility. After his
retirement, two of the players who have marked the
international competition have different profiles.
Alfie Hewett has Perthes disease, a hip disorder that
significantly affects the function of his lower limbs.
He can walk, but his impairment prevents him from
competing on equal terms in conventional tennis.
The case of Tokito Oda is different. After being
diagnosed with osteosarcoma at age nine, he lost some
of the function of a lower limb. His disability meets the
criteria required to compete in wheelchair tennis, but
he does not respond to the classic profile of spinal cord
injury either.
It would be methodologically incorrect to interpret this
sequence as evidence of structural change. Kunieda,
Hewett and Oda belong to different generations, and
each brings together a wide set of technical-tactical,
physical and psychological skills and qualities.
The case of Hewett adds a particularly interesting
element. During the review of the classification
criteria conducted between 2019 and 2021, there was
a possibility that her disability would no longer meet
the minimum threshold necessary to participate. After
a lengthy review and appeal process, the player finally
maintained his eligibility.
This episode highlights the difficulty of establishing
where the Minimum Impairment Criterion (MIC) should
be located. There is no simple functional boundary
between who should and who should not compete. Nor
can it be assumed that all people above the MIC have an
equivalent impact of their disability on performance.
Here we find a relevant distinction. The MIC mainly
answers the question of who is eligible. The question
we pose in this article is different: among all those
who meet the criterion, does functional severity still
have enough influence on performance to modify the
probabilities of reaching the top positions?
The inclusion-exclusion paradox
Wheelchair tennis emerged as an opportunity for
people with significant mobility limitations to practice
and compete in the sport of tennis. For many years,
especially for those with a spinal cord injury, it was one
of the few ways to develop a competitive career within
the sport. We believe that the current evolution poses a
possible paradox.

Another aspect to mention, and especially relevant
in wheelchair tennis, is that of mobility. De Groot
et al. (2016) and Rietveld et al. (2019) observed that
different displacement, acceleration and agility tests
allow discriminating between performance levels.
Subsequent work, using inertial measurement units,
has made it possible to more accurately characterize
mobility during real matches (Rietveld et al., 2023).
Here we find one of the central arguments of our
proposal. If mobility differentiates competitive levels
and today's tennis demands increasingly faster and
more efficient movements, we need to know how
much that mobility depends on the player's residual
functional capacity.
The study by van der Slikke et al. (2026) provides
particularly relevant information. In a sample of 51
elite players grouped according to three levels of trunk
function, athletes with greater functional capacity
achieved higher speeds, accelerations and rotational
capacities during specific mobility.
The trunk participates directly in basic actions of
wheelchair tennis. It helps to stabilize the body during
propulsion, allows rotations to be generated during
strokes and makes it easier to regain balance after
hitting situations. For all these reasons, a greater
functional capacity of the trunk will be a mechanical
advantage for movements and hits.
We also know that specific physical factors condition
the speed of the serve (Sánchez-Pay et al., 2021c) and
that there are differences in the effectiveness of some
shots between high-level winners and losers (SánchezPay et al., 2017).
We are not yet facing a proven causal relationship. The
available evidence does allow us to establish a plausible
sequence: the game has become more demanding;
mobility has a growing weight; the function of the trunk
conditions part of this mobility; and these capabilities
contribute to technical and competitive performance.
If all these characteristics gain importance, which
functional profiles will have the greatest chance of
reaching the elite?

Functional equity and integration in
professional tennis
On the other hand, there is a second issue that probably
needs to be analysed together with the previous one.
Wheelchair tennis has achieved something that few
Paralympic sports have achieved: integrating itself
into the very structure of conventional professional
sport. All four Grand Slams currently include chair
competition, and similar experiences are beginning
to appear at ATP and WTA events. This restructuring
of the UNIQLO Wheelchair Tennis Tour announced by
World Tennis will divide the professional circuit into
three levels: the development, the international and
the Premier Tour (the highest level). The Premier Tour
includes Grand Slams, year-end Masters and integrated
tournaments classified as follows:
•	 Wheelchair 1000 Events: Integrated into ATP
and WTA Masters 1000 tournaments (such as the
Miami Open, which already operated under this
model in a pioneering way).
•	 Wheelchair 500 Events: Coupled to ATP/WTA 500
tournaments.
•	 Wheelchair 250 Events: Coupled to ATP/WTA 250
tournaments.
This integration (which includes at least seven
additional integrated tournaments in 2026) provides
obvious benefits. Players compete in prestigious venues,
media coverage and visibility of the sport increases,
commercial opportunities grow, and the public
perceives wheelchair tennis as part of professional
tennis.
Given this situation, it would be appropriate to ask how
important the simplicity of the competitive system
has been, where only two divisions are proposed at the
functional level (Open and Quad).
This structure makes it possible to organize relatively
small paintings that are understandable to all audiences
and audiences. For years, some Grand Slams worked
with just eight players per category, a figure that shows
the logistical limitations that exist when an additional
competition is incorporated into an already complex
event.
Possibly if a more detailed classification were
implemented, it could increase functional homogeneity
among the participants, but it would also probably
imply more categories, more draws, more matches and
greater needs for courts and programming, complicating
the logistics and management of resources for the
Tournament.
There is no evidence to support World Tennis
maintaining the current model for this purpose. Nor
have we found documentation that establishes a
causal relationship between the classification system
and professional integration. We pose it only as a
A system designed to include could generate, without
intending to, a form of competitive selection in which
some of the profiles with the greatest functional
impairment would have increasingly difficulty reaching
the sporting elite and, therefore, could be excluded.
We are not talking about normative exclusion. A person
with a spinal cord injury who meets the requirements
can compete today in the Open Division just as they did
twenty years ago. Nor do we suggest that players with
less impairment have an absolute advantage or that
their performance can be explained by their disability.
Rather, understanding that high sports performance in
wheelchair tennis requires slightly higher acceleration,
greater stability during hits in stable and/or unstable
positions, faster recovery, among other factors, these
differences between some players and others depending
on residual body function, could condition the sports
evolution over time.
In addition, the evolution of the game itself could
intensify this dynamic. The higher the speed of the
competition, the more value capabilities such as
acceleration, power, travel efficiency and trunk control
acquire. In this scenario, small functional differences
that twenty years ago were perhaps less decisive could
have a greater influence today.
This
interpretation
remains
a
hypothesis.
To
demonstrate this, we need to reconstruct the functional
profile of the ranking during different historical periods
and study whether the proportion of athletes with less
impact in their top positions has really increased over
time.
Even without such longitudinal evidence yet, the issue
has practical implications. One of them appears in the
introductory programs to wheelchair tennis developed
in hospitals and rehabilitation centres. For a person
who has just contracted a spinal cord injury, meeting
other players with similar experiences and functional
abilities can have a strong identifying effect.
For decades, many international leaders had profiles
clearly associated with spinal cord injuries. If those
in the top positions progressively show different
functional characteristics, there could be a certain
distance between the new practitioner and the elite
model he observes.
This does not reduce the rehabilitative, social or sporting
value of wheelchair tennis. Tennis can improve physical
condition, promote autonomy, improve socialization
and provide a competitive activity regardless of the
level achieved.
The question is in the message we convey. Perhaps
recruitment programs should not rely solely on the
figures of the world's Top 10-15 as a representation of
what it means to practice wheelchair tennis. Showcasing
athletes with different functional levels and different
competitive goals can offer a broader and more realistic
picture of sport.

Finally, the bodies responsible for sport could
incorporate this issue into future classification reviews.
Not starting from the idea that the current system is
necessarily unfair, but periodically asking whether the

Limitations and future lines of research
The main limitation of this work derives from its very
nature. We present a narrative review and a working
hypothesis, not a longitudinal review.
We do not yet have a systematic analysis that allows us
to affirm that players with less functional impairment
have increased their representation in the top positions.
The examples used have a high illustrative value, but
they do not replace a complete reconstruction of the
ranking. We have based ourselves on the lists of players
who reached the quarterfinals at the Paralympic Games
from 1992 to Paris 2024 in the men's Open category
and the players in this category to reach the top of the
rankings in the last 10 years without considering the
women's or the Quad category.
We should also avoid identifying clinical diagnosis with
functional capacity. A spinal cord injury, an amputation
or a sequel of cancer does not define by themselves how
a player moves, how much trunk control he retains or
what performance he can develop.
The available scientific literature is still relatively
limited and uses small samples, which is understandable
in high-level Paralympic sport. Williamson et al. (2024)
also noted considerable heterogeneity among the
protocols used to study physical demands.
We
need,
therefore,
longitudinal
analyses
and
standardized systems of functional assessment that
allow us to relate disability, specific activity and
competitive performance more directly.
The institutional hypothesis on professional integration
presents an additional limitation. The coexistence
between a simple qualification and a high level of
integration in major tournaments does not allow
causality to be established. Studying it would require
incorporating the perspective of organisers, World
Tennis, Grand Slams, players and other stakeholders.

From a strictly functional perspective, it might be
desirable to reduce the differences between players
competing for the same titles, however, from the
perspective of the organization of the circuit,
maintaining few categories favours a compact format
and possibly facilitates their presence within the major
tournaments.
It does not seem appropriate to pose it as a binary
choice between equity or integration and probably,
intermediate solutions could be implemented, such
as the study of differentiated participation systems,
or even alternative classification systems without
necessarily if each functional level must generate an
independent table.
However, this type of proposal will require a greater
study of the quality and quantity of the data offered by
the circuit and its history.
Implications for the development of
wheelchair tennis
The first priority should be to know if the phenomenon
in question really exists and what its magnitude is.
In this sense, we propose to carry out a review study
on the World Top 20 at different times (for example,
2010, 2015, 2020 and 2025) to later relate each player
with sufficiently documented clinical and functional
information. This would make it possible to analyse
whether the distribution of profiles has changed
and whether there is any temporal association with
regulatory modifications or with the evolution of
performance itself.
It would also be interesting to complement clinical
diagnoses with functional measures. Two athletes with
the same aetiology can have very different abilities. To
really understand the effect on performance we need
data on trunk function, strength, stability, mobility and
propulsion capacity, beyond the name of the injury.
From the point of view of training, this line of research
can help to better individualize physical and technical
preparation. Players with less core control may need
specific saddle configuration strategies, stability
work or technical-tactical adaptation that reduce the
disadvantage associated with certain actions.
In development programmes it would also be
interesting to follow players with different functional
profiles longitudinally. It is not enough to know who
makes it to the Top 10. We need to know who starts the
sport, who quits, who progresses and at what point the
biggest differences appear.
